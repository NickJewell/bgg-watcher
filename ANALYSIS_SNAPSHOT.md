# Trick-taking design analysis — 2026-11-21

Generated by a scheduled Claude Code session from the 20 most active games in the 2026-11-21 snapshot. 100 variants were generated and scored; the top 10 are pitched below. Scores are the model's judgement against a fixed rubric, useful for sorting ideas, not a forecast.

**Source games:** Schadenfreude, Apocalypse, Minikin City, Moustache, Potemkin Villages, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Last Lemming, Shards of Creation, Oshaka Trick, Fishing 2, Can't Win, Trick to the Future, Trick Stakes, Zombie Princess, Matchmakers, Tax the Rich, Off With Their Heads!, Rebel Princess, All Mine!, Mythical Dice

## Top mechanics

- **Tricks you would rather not win** (Schadenfreude, Rebel Princess, Zombie Princess, Apocalypse, Oshaka Trick, Last Lemming): Six of the twenty now punish winning in some way, and Schadenfreude's weekly plays have climbed for a third snapshot running, to 868. Rebel Princess returning to the list beside its sequel shows that the Hearts family, where the fun is in what you are forced to take, still has the broadest pull.
- **A fresh rule every round** (Rebel Princess, Zombie Princess, Moustache, Last Lemming): Each of these plays a short series of rounds with a different rule card on top. A game of four or five rounds never repeats its puzzle, the base rules can stay very small, and the round card gives the table something to talk about before the first lead.
- **Predicting the shape of your haul** (All Mine!, Can't Win, Mythical Dice, Tax the Rich): All Mine! asks which treasure you will take most and least of, not how many tricks. Like Can't Win's chosen constraint, it replaces the bare number with a statement about what kind of hand you hold, which is easier for a newcomer to reason about and harder to solve.
- **Tricks that move pieces on a board** (Last Lemming, Trick Stakes, Minikin City, Off With Their Heads!): Last Lemming turns each trick into a shove toward a cliff edge. Putting the consequence of a trick on a track or a sheet makes the state of the game visible at a glance and lets a card game look like a board game on the shelf.
- **Cooperation with personal tasks** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Potemkin Villages): The Fellowship game is still the second most played title here and by far the most owned. Different jobs for each seat inside one shared hand produce teamwork without a leader, because nobody can see enough to give orders.
- **Captured cards as material** (Fishing 2, Matchmakers, Schadenfreude, Shards of Creation, All Mine!): When what is inside the trick matters more than the trick itself, every card another player throws in is aimed at somebody. It gives the player who cannot follow suit a decision instead of a discard.
- **Short powers for characters and suits** (Rebel Princess, Zombie Princess, Potemkin Villages, Moustache, Shards of Creation, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game): A princess, a villager or a suit with one special sentence gives each player an identity and each game a different mix. It is also what lets a design carry a cast of known characters.
- **Teams that change** (Moustache, Tax the Rich, Zombie Princess, Matchmakers): Partnerships reshuffled each round bring the warmth of playing with someone to tables of three, five or six, and they stop one strong pair from running away with the game.
- **Tokens that bend a card's value** (Trick to the Future, Minikin City): A small public stock of fuel or workers that alters a card gives every hand some control over its luck, and gives opponents something to count besides cards.

## Top themes

- **Fairy tales and licensed fantasy** (Rebel Princess, Zombie Princess, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Shards of Creation, Off With Their Heads!): The two most-owned games in the set are a Tolkien licence and a fairy-tale princess game. Characters the buyer already knows do the work of explaining who has which power.
- **Comic disaster** (Last Lemming, Apocalypse, Zombie Princess, Oshaka Trick, Schadenfreude): Lemmings over a cliff, the end of the world, zombie princes: a theme that announces things will go badly makes a punishing rule into a joke the table shares.
- **Animals** (Last Lemming, Moustache, Fishing 2, Matchmakers): Animal casts make chaotic rules feel friendly and are the easiest art direction to sell to families. The group grew this week with Last Lemming.
- **Treasure, races and wagers** (All Mine!, Trick Stakes, Mythical Dice, Last Lemming): Mining claims, horse racing, dice battles and a rush for the cliff all give the hand a visible prize or finish line, which helps players who find a bare trick count abstract.
- **Class and politics** (Tax the Rich, Potemkin Villages, Off With Their Heads!): Rank is the native subject of playing cards, so stories about hierarchy and its overthrow explain both the ordinary rule and the exception.
- **Building something small** (Minikin City, Potemkin Villages): A little city or village left in front of each player at the end gives a sense of having made something, which a pile of tricks does not.

## Top differentiators

- **Second-highest wins** (Schadenfreude): Still the hottest game in the snapshot, on one changed sentence about who takes the trick. It levels experienced and new players because nobody's habits apply.
- **Going too far is fatal** (Last Lemming, Schadenfreude, Trick to the Future): A cliff edge, a forty-point ceiling, a fuel supply that can strand you: when success past a certain point becomes failure, the leader is the player in most danger and every late trick is a real question.
- **Suits printed on the card backs** (All Mine!): All Mine! shows each card's treasure type on its back, so everyone can see what suits you hold and where you are void. It costs nothing to print and shifts the game from memory toward open reading of the table.
- **Most-and-least predictions** (All Mine!): Naming the category you will collect most and least of is a bid a beginner can make by looking at their hand, yet it gives two separate things to steer toward during play.
- **A handicap you choose** (Can't Win, All Mine!): A restriction or prediction selected after seeing your cards makes the deal into a decision, and makes a bad round your own doing.
- **Shared doom tokens** (Apocalypse, Potemkin Villages, Last Lemming): A countdown or an edge that everyone approaches together gives rivals a common worry and produces brinkmanship without direct attacks.
- **Hands and catches that carry over** (Trick Stakes, Fishing 2): Keeping your hand all game, or playing next round with what you caught, lets players learn their own cards over time and see their improvement from round to round.
- **A sequel line** (Rebel Princess, Zombie Princess, Fishing 2): Rebel Princess is back in the list three years after release, pulled up by its sequel, and Fishing 2 follows Fishing. A base system that takes a new rule deck or a new cast gives a publisher a second and third box.
- **Works at two** (Minikin City, Potemkin Villages, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Shards of Creation, Trick to the Future, Matchmakers, Off With Their Heads!): Seven of the twenty list two players, and no dedicated two-player game is in the set this week. A trick-taker that is honest at two still has little competition.
- **Props beyond the deck** (Last Lemming, Mythical Dice, Trick Stakes, Off With Their Heads!, Apocalypse, Trick to the Future): Lemming figures, dice, a race track, a score sheet and flipping tokens give small card games a look of their own and something to handle between plays.

### 1. Don't Feed the Bear — 75.2/100

*A bear sits at the table and plays its best card every time. If it wins the most tricks, nobody wins.*

- **Core twist:** A bear hand lies face-up and plays automatically in turn: always its highest legal card. Players score a point per trick as usual, but if the bear ends the round with the most tricks, nobody scores. Stopping the bear costs good cards, and everyone would rather someone else paid.
- **Theme:** A campsite with a hungry bear
- **Remixes:** Apocalypse, Last Lemming, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Shared doom tokens, Animals
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 7, mechanics 8, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** A face-up automaton that always plays its highest card and voids the round if it wins most creates a readable public-goods standoff with a funny theme, but the trailing player has an incentive to feed the bear and spoil the round.

Don't Feed the Bear seats an extra player at the table: a bear whose hand lies face-up for everyone to read. Play is must-follow with one trump suit and nine tricks. When the bear's turn comes, whoever is to its left plays its card for it, and the rule is fixed: the bear plays its highest card of the led suit, or its highest trump if it has none, or its highest card otherwise. Players score a point per trick. If the bear finishes with more tricks than any player, the round is void and nobody scores. The decision is in every trick the bear is about to win: someone has to spend a card big enough to stop it, and everyone can see exactly which card that is and who holds something that would do.

Because the bear is predictable, it can be played against, and that is what players learn. Early on they simply overpower it and resent the cost. Then they see that the bear always burns its best card first, so leading a suit where it holds one high card and two low ones strips it for the price of one trick. They learn to feed it tricks nobody wants, to count how many it can still win, and to judge when a rival is close enough to the bear's total that helping the bear hurts the rival more. The dynamic is a public-goods standoff with no hidden information about the threat, and the feeling is the comic exasperation of a picnic being raided while everyone waits for someone else to stand up.

A campsite bear is a cover that sells to families, the game is one deck and a bear card, and it works from 2 to 4 because the bear supplies a full extra seat; at two it is close to a three-handed game. The scorer's risk is the spoiler: a player who is behind gains from a void round. I would playtest scoring a void round as minus one for everyone except the player with the fewest tricks, which removes the incentive without adding much text, and compare it with letting the leader on the scoreboard lose a point in a void round. The number to track is how often a round is voided; around one in five keeps the threat real without making the evening feel wasted.

### 2. Piggy Bank — 72.2/100

*The tricks you win aren't safe until you lose one on purpose.*

- **Core twist:** Tricks you win sit unbanked in front of you. Whenever you play the lowest card of the led suit in a trick, you bank everything you have unbanked. If you win the final trick of the round, all your unbanked tricks are lost. You have to choose when to stop winning.
- **Theme:** Children saving pocket money
- **Remixes:** Oshaka Trick, Schadenfreude, Last Lemming, Going too far is fatal, Tricks you would rather not win
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 8, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Banking unbanked tricks only by playing the lowest card of the led suit, with the last trick wiping the greedy, is a true push-your-luck loop expressed through card play, but 'lowest of led suit' can be denied by others in frustrating ways.

In Piggy Bank the tricks you win are not yet yours. A round is ten tricks, must-follow, with one trump suit. Each trick you take is stacked face-down in front of you, unbanked. The only way to bank is to lose in a particular way: whenever you play the lowest card of the led suit in a trick, everything in front of you goes into your piggy bank and is safe. Whoever wins the tenth and last trick loses every trick they still have unbanked. Banked tricks are a point each. The central decision is when to stop: you have three tricks out and a 2 of clubs in hand, and the question is whether clubs will be led again before the end and whether someone will slip a lower card under you when it is.

The pattern to learn is that low cards are keys and must be kept as carefully as aces. New players win tricks happily and are wiped out at the end; the next time they hoard low cards and find that a 3 is not the lowest when an opponent holds the 2. With experience comes counting the bottom of each suit as closely as the top, banking small and often when the low cards are uncertain, and leading a suit specifically to deny a rival the low spot. The last three tricks are tense in a way ordinary trick-taking is not, because the strongest hand is the one most at risk. At the table it gives the cheering and groaning of a push-your-luck dice game, with the difference that the other players, not the dice, decide whether you get out.

Saving up and losing the lot is a story every child understands, the components are a deck and a bank card each, and the rule is two sentences. It is for 3 to 4 players. The risk the scorer identifies is that being denied the low spot can feel arbitrary. I would test allowing a bank on the lowest card of any suit in the trick, which makes it easier, and separately a once-per-round hammer token that banks at any time, then keep whichever leaves bust rates at about one player per round. I would also check that the leader on points is busted more often than others; if not, the catch-up is not working.

### 3. Herding Cats — 72.0/100

*Every trick nudges the cat one step. Everyone wants it in a different basket.*

- **Core twist:** A cat token starts in the centre of a cross-shaped board whose four arms match the four suits. After each trick the cat moves one step along the arm of the winning card's suit. Each player holds a secret basket card naming an arm. When the cat reaches the end of an arm the round stops and anyone with that basket scores five; tricks score one each.
- **Theme:** A cat nobody can control
- **Remixes:** Last Lemming, Trick Stakes, Moustache, Tricks that move pieces on a board, Shared doom tokens
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 6, mechanics 7, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Making the winning card's suit (not its owner) steer a shared cat towards secret baskets creates natural reads and tug-of-war with a great one-line hook, though with two or three players unowned arms can make the pull feel lopsided or arbitrary.

Herding Cats puts a small cross-shaped board in the middle of the table with a cat token at its centre. Each arm is four steps long and marked with one of the four suits. Every player is dealt a secret basket card naming one arm. Hands are must-follow with no trump. After each trick, the cat walks one step out along the arm of the winning card's suit, or one step back toward the centre and then out if it was on a different arm. When the cat reaches the end of an arm the round ends: every player whose basket is there scores five, and each trick won scores one. The decision on each card is therefore double. Winning is worth a point, but what you win with moves the cat, and it may be moving it toward someone else's basket.

What players learn is to read intentions from suits. Somebody who keeps winning in blue probably wants blue, unless they are holding the cat away from red. Two players may share a basket without knowing it and pull together for a while, then notice. Players learn to win with a harmless suit when they only want the point, to lose a trick so that a rival is forced to win it in the wrong colour, and to bluff early so that their real arm is not obvious when it matters. Since the cat's position is plain to see, every trick has a visible result even for the people who lost it. The table dynamic is a four-way tug of war with whispered accusations, and the feeling is the comedy the title promises: nobody is in control and everyone is trying.

The box line explains the game, a cat is the most reliable animal in the hobby for selling a small box, and the parts are a deck, a folded board and a wooden cat. It plays 2 to 4. The scorer's risk is real: with fewer than four, some arms belong to nobody and the pull can feel arbitrary. I would playtest dealing each player two baskets at two and three players, scoring five for the first and three for the second, so that every arm matters to someone. I would also test whether a round reliably ends inside ten tricks, and shorten the arms to three steps if the cat too often never arrives.

### 4. Last Orders — 71.2/100

*Ring the bell and the next trick is the last. Just don't win it.*

- **Core twist:** Tricks score a point each. Any player who has just won a trick may ring the bell before leading: the trick they now lead is the final one of the round, and whoever wins it loses three points. Unplayed cards are thrown in. The bell-ringer has to lead something they are sure to lose.
- **Theme:** Closing time at a pub
- **Remixes:** Oshaka Trick, Apocalypse, Last Lemming, Going too far is fatal, Tricks you would rather not win
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** A player-called final trick that costs its winner three gives a dramatic, theme-perfect ending and a real timing decision, but early bells can shorten rounds and strand players who never got to play their hand.

Last Orders is ordinary trick-taking with an ending the players choose. Ten cards are dealt, play is must-follow with a trump suit, and each trick is worth a point. A brass bell, or a card standing in for one, sits in the middle. Any player who has just won a trick may ring it before leading. The trick they then lead is the last of the round; whoever wins it loses three points, and all cards still in hand are thrown in. If nobody rings, the tenth trick is the last one by default and carries the same penalty. The decision is when to call time. You must have just won a trick to ring, and you must then lead a card you are confident someone will have to beat.

The skill that grows is holding a safe exit. Players learn to keep one low card in a suit that the others still hold, and to notice when an opponent no longer has one, since a player with only high cards cannot afford to ring and cannot afford to be led to either. They learn to ring early when they are ahead on tricks and the others are sitting on unplayed winners, and to deny the bell by keeping a likely ringer from winning a trick at all. Each round has a different length, which changes the worth of every card: an ace saved for later may never be played. The feeling is the scramble of closing time, with a sharp final beat in which three or four people try to lose one trick.

Pub closing time is a theme adults recognise instantly, the bell is a cheap and memorable prop, and the rules add two sentences to a game most people half know. It is for 3 to 4 players. The scorer's concern is early bells stranding players with unplayed hands. I would playtest forbidding the bell before the fourth trick, and paying each player one point for every two cards still in hand when it rings, so that a short round is not a blank for those who were waiting. The measure is average round length; I want it around seven tricks, with the full ten happening rarely enough to be an event.

### 5. Crescendo — 70.5/100

*The first trick is worth one, the second two, the seventh seven. Then the conductor drops the baton.*

- **Core twist:** A round has eight tricks. The first is worth one point, the second two, and so on up to seven for the seventh. The eighth and last trick is worth nothing. You want your best cards late, but not last, and so does everyone else.
- **Theme:** An orchestra building to a finale
- **Remixes:** Oshaka Trick, Last Lemming, Trick Stakes, Going too far is fatal
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 9, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Tricks worth one to seven then a worthless eighth is a single-sentence rule that builds a shared rising tension and a game of chicken over the seventh, but early tricks become filler and one late trick can decide the round.

Crescendo changes one thing: what each trick is worth. A round is eight tricks from a hand of eight, must-follow, with a trump suit. A track on the table shows the values rising from one for the first trick to seven for the seventh, and a zero for the eighth. That is the whole rule. The decision it creates runs through the entire hand. Early tricks are cheap, so they are the place to shed awkward cards and to draw out other people's strength. The sixth and seventh are where the points are. But every card you save for them must be one you can still play to effect when they arrive, and whoever wins the seventh has to lead the worthless eighth with whatever is left.

Players learn to think of the hand backwards. First they hold their aces for the end and discover that a saved ace is useless if its suit has been run out or trumped by then. Then they start managing entries: winning a cheap early trick on purpose to lead the suit that will leave them in control at trick six, keeping a trump for the seventh, and working out which opponent is saving what from the cards they refuse to play. The single worthless trick at the end matters more than it looks, because everyone's final card is forced, and good players arrange for it to be a card they never wanted. The dynamic is a slow build with everyone visibly holding back, and the feeling matches the name: quiet, then loud.

Nothing could be easier to teach or cheaper to make, a deck and a strip of card, and it works unchanged from 2 to 4, with two players each also playing from a short face-up row. The scorer's worries are that early tricks are filler and that one late trick decides the round. I would playtest a flatter curve, such as one, one, two, two, three, three, five, zero, and a bonus of three for winning the most tricks overall, so that early wins count toward something. I would measure the share of round winners who also won the seventh trick, and adjust the curve until it falls below about sixty percent.

### 6. Loser Buys the Round — 69.8/100

*Everybody collects penalties. Only the one with the most pays for them.*

- **Core twist:** Tricks score a point each. Penalty cards are counted at the end of the round, and only the player holding the most of them loses points, one per card. Everyone else's penalties are forgiven. If players tie for most, they all pay.
- **Theme:** Friends at the pub
- **Remixes:** Rebel Princess, Schadenfreude, Oshaka Trick, Tricks you would rather not win, Going too far is fatal
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 7, mechanics 8, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Only the single biggest penalty-holder pays, so the table plays chicken and gangs up on whoever is ahead in the count with one clean rule, though a targeted player can feel bullied and it needs three or more.

Loser Buys the Round is a penalty game in which almost all the penalties are forgiven. The deck has four suits; one of them, twelve cards, is the bar tab. Play is must-follow with no trump and tricks score a point each. Captured tab cards are kept face-up. At the end of the round only the player holding the most tab cards pays, losing a point for each one, and everybody else's are wiped clean; players tied for most all pay. The decision is how close to the edge to stand. Taking a trick with two tab cards in it is free as long as someone else ends up with more, so the real question on every trick is not whether it is bad but whether it makes you the worst.

The lesson is relative position. Beginners avoid the penalty suit entirely and score few tricks. Then they notice that a player holding three tab cards with another on five is perfectly safe and can win freely, and that the player on five is now everyone's dumping ground. Skilled play is about staying one behind the leader in penalties while taking as many tricks as possible, and about engineering a late swing so that the count changes hands on the last trick or two. Since every count is face-up the feedback is clear, and the table's attention shifts round by round to whoever is carrying the most. It produces loud, good-humoured ganging up, and the theme gives it the right tone: someone is going to pay and it would be nice if it were not you.

The hook is a phrase people already use, a single deck is all it needs, and it suits 3 to 4 players. The scorer warns that a targeted player can feel bullied. I would playtest two softeners: the payer's loss capped at five, and the payer leading the first trick of the next round and passing two cards of their choice to the player who took the fewest penalties. I would also watch whether the same person pays twice in a row more often than chance would predict; if so, the hands need a pass before play to let a weak holding be fixed.

### 7. Spinning Plates — 69.6/100

*Three plates are wobbling. Every trick saves one and lets the others slip.*

- **Core twist:** Cooperative. Three of the four suits each have a plate on a track starting at four. After every trick each plate drops one step, except the plate of the winning card's suit, which goes back to four. A plate at zero smashes and the team loses. The fourth suit is trump and saves nothing. Survive ten tricks.
- **Theme:** A circus plate-spinning act
- **Remixes:** The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Apocalypse, Last Lemming, Shared doom tokens, Cooperation with personal tasks
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 7, dynamics 6, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Three decaying suit plates that reset only when their suit wins give a visible, escalating co-op tension that matches the theme exactly, but one fixed scenario could become a solved rotation without variable difficulty.

Spinning Plates is cooperative. Three of the four suits each have a plate on a short track, starting at four. The fourth suit is trump. Players are dealt ten cards, may not discuss them, and play must-follow tricks. After every trick each plate drops one step, except that if the trick was won by a card of a plate's suit, that plate goes back to four. A plate that reaches zero smashes and the team has lost; if all three are still up after ten tricks, the team has won. The decision each trick is which plate to save, and it belongs to everyone: the leader chooses a suit, but the trick is saved only if a card of the needed colour actually wins it, and a trump played to win saves nothing.

The arithmetic is simple and unforgiving, and that is what players learn. A plate lasts four tricks, there are three plates, so the team has one spare trick in every four and must spend it wisely. Players discover that the rotation breaks whenever the wrong person holds the lead, that a void lets you discard but never rescue, and that trump is a temptation: it wins the trick and lets all three plates fall. They learn to show partners where their strength is by what they play early, and to keep a winner in each colour for the late tricks when suits are running thin. The tracks make every failure easy to trace. The feeling is shared, rising panic of a pleasant kind, with a cheer when the last plate is caught.

Cooperative trick-taking is the proven route to a wide audience in this snapshot, plate-spinning explains the rule in a picture, and the box is a deck and three small tracks, for 2 to 4 players. The scorer's risk is that one fixed set-up becomes a solved rotation. I would build a ladder of twelve acts and playtest each: plates starting at different heights, a fourth plate, a wobble card that drops one plate two steps, a clown who must win no tricks. The test is whether a group that clears an act immediately wants the next, and whether win rates fall steadily from about eighty percent on the first act to about thirty on the last.

### 8. Own Goal — 68.0/100

*Win a trick you led yourself and it counts against you.*

- **Core twist:** A trick you win when following scores a point. A trick you win with the card you led costs you a point. The lead becomes something to get rid of, and a leader's best card is one that somebody will be forced to beat.
- **Theme:** Sunday-league football
- **Remixes:** Schadenfreude, Oshaka Trick, Last Lemming, Tricks you would rather not win
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 7, mechanics 8, dynamics 7, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 6
- **Scorer's note:** Penalising a trick won with your own lead is a single rule that inverts the value of the lead and teaches 'lead to be beaten', but the sign flip trips new players and opponents ducking to stick the leader can turn play negative.

Own Goal has one rule beyond the basics. Hands are nine cards, must-follow, with no trump. If you win a trick while following, you score a point. If the card you led wins the trick, you lose a point. The winner of a trick leads the next, as usual. So the lead, normally the reward for winning, is a liability: you must now put down a card that someone else will beat. The decision for the leader is which card can be relied on to lose, and for everyone else it is whether to beat it. Ducking under the lead on purpose, so that it wins and costs its owner a point, is always an option, at the price of not scoring yourself.

That tension is what players learn to manage. The first discovery is to lead low. The second is that low leads can be ducked by anyone holding a lower card, so the safest lead is not your lowest card but one in a suit where you know a higher card must be played. Then comes the real game: when you follow and could win, taking the point also takes the lead, and you need an exit card ready before you accept it. Players start counting who has been run out of low cards and steering the lead to them near the end. Every outcome is easy to trace back to a choice, and the table dynamic is a string of small traps and escapes. The feeling is the rueful comedy of the theme, the ball going in off your own defender.

It costs one deck, takes a sentence to teach to anyone who has played a trick-taker, and the football framing travels everywhere, for 3 to 4 players. The scorer's two risks are the sign flip confusing new players and rounds going negative when everyone ducks. I would playtest giving the leader a point if nobody follows suit at all, and a version in which an own goal costs nothing but gives a point to each other player, which keeps scores positive and may be easier to grasp. I would have first-time players explain the rule back after one hand; if fewer than nine in ten can, the positive-scoring version wins.

### 9. Slips and Ladders — 67.6/100

*The card you win with is the number you move, so pick the one that lands on a ladder.*

- **Core twist:** Each player has a pawn on a forty-space board of ladders and slides. Cards run from one to six in four suits. The trick winner moves forward exactly the rank of their winning card and then follows any ladder or slide they land on. Nobody else moves.
- **Theme:** A playground of slides and climbing frames
- **Remixes:** Last Lemming, Trick Stakes, Tricks that move pieces on a board, Going too far is fatal
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 5, learning feedback 7, mechanics 8, dynamics 5, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Moving exactly the rank you won with turns a universally known children's board into a choice about which card to win with, but only the winner moves and a forty-space track with ranks one to six risks a long, low-interaction race.

Slips and Ladders joins a trick-taking hand to a board every family already owns in some form. The board has forty numbered spaces with ladders up and slides down. Cards run from one to six in four suits, with one suit as trump, and each player holds eight. Tricks are must-follow. Only the winner of a trick moves, and they move exactly the rank of the card they won with, then take any ladder or slide they land on. The first pawn to reach the last space wins. The central decision is which card to win with. A 6 wins more easily but may land on a slide; a 2 lands on the ladder, if you can find a trick that a 2 will win.

The learning is in connecting the hand to the board. Players start by winning with whatever they can and are sent down slides. Soon they are counting the spaces ahead, noticing that they need exactly a three, and working out how to win a trick with their 3 of trumps or by leading a suit the others are out of. They learn to read opponents' pawns as well: a rival who needs a four to hit the long ladder should not be allowed to win with one, and can be forced to overtake with a higher card instead. Because movement is rare and exact, each trick won is an event, and the cause of every slide is obvious. The dynamic is a race with plenty of denial, and the feeling is the familiar lurch of the children's game with the difference that you chose the number.

This is the most immediately explicable design on the list for non-gamers and for children old enough to follow suit, and it works from 2 to 4. The scorer's risks are that only the winner moves and that forty spaces is long. I would playtest a thirty-space board, and a rule that the player of the lowest card moves one space, so that losing still does something and can itself reach a ladder. I would time games with families and aim for fifteen minutes; if hands regularly end with nobody near the finish, the board is too long or the ladders too few.

### 10. Head Above Water — 66.9/100

*The tide rises one step each trick. Play anything below it and you go under.*

- **Core twist:** Cooperative. A tide marker starts at one and rises by one after every trick. Any card played with a rank below the tide costs the team one of three lifebelts. Low cards must be used early, while the lead and the need to follow suit conspire to keep them in hand.
- **Theme:** Seaside lifeguards
- **Remixes:** The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Apocalypse, Last Lemming, Shared doom tokens, Going too far is fatal
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 5, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** A tide that rises one rank per trick is an instantly legible clock that makes low cards urgent, but late-round losses are forced by the deal and the puzzle may repeat itself.

Head Above Water is a cooperative game about getting rid of small cards before they drown you. The deck has four suits ranked 1 to 10 with one trump suit, and each player holds eight cards. A tide marker sits on a track at one and moves up a step after every trick. Any card played to a trick with a rank lower than the tide costs the team one of its three lifebelts. Players may not discuss their hands. The team wins by finishing the eighth trick with a lifebelt left. Following suit is compulsory, so the decision is constantly about which low cards can be played now, while they are still legal, and who has to hold the lead so that the right suits are opened in time.

The pattern is clear from the first hand and takes several to handle well. By the fifth trick a 4 is a liability and by the eighth so is a 7, so everyone wants to play low to high, yet somebody has to win each trick and lead the next. Players learn that winning a trick with a middling card early is a service to the team, that leading a suit lets three partners unload their small cards in it at once, and that being void is valuable because a discard can be the low card nothing else would shift. They also learn to recognise a hand that cannot be saved entirely and to decide together, without words, where to spend a lifebelt. The rising marker makes the pressure visible, and the feeling is of a team working against a clock that never speeds up and never stops.

The seaside theme is friendly, the rule is one sentence, and the box needs only a deck, a tide track and three tokens, for 2 to 4 players. The scorer points out that some deals force late losses and that the puzzle may repeat. I would playtest a pass of one card to the left before play, which gives a team some repair, and a set of beach cards that vary the rule: a tide that rises two on the third trick, a rock pool where one suit is exempt, a spring tide starting at two. I would record how many lifebelts the best possible play would have lost on each deal, and tune the hand size so that unwinnable deals are rare.
