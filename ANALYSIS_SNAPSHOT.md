## Top mechanics

- **Shed your hand by beating the last play** (Woo, LOOP, Pipoca, Crisps!, Bridge City Poker, Dickory, Haunted Mouse, Flamenco): All eight ladder-climbing sources share one skeleton: play a single, a set or a run, the next player beats it or passes, and the aim is an empty hand. Because the skeleton is so fixed, each game is defined by one bend in it, which makes this family the easiest of the three to design a clean one-rule variant for.
- **Flip for more or stop** (Port Royal, Port Royal: Big Box, Death Valley, Don't Go In There, YRO): Port Royal has nearly thirty thousand owners on a rule a child can state: turn over another card or keep what you have. The decision needs no card-game experience at all, which is why push-your-luck travels further beyond the hobby than trick-taking does.
- **Risk that other players set for you** (Honeypot, L'oaf, Don't Go In There): In Honeypot an opponent stacks the pile you dig through, and in L'oaf everyone's effort is hidden until it is counted. Moving the danger from the deck to the other players turns a solitary gamble into bluffing and reading people.
- **Tricks you would rather not win** (Schadenfreude, Apocalypse): Schadenfreude's weekly plays rose again, to 896, and it stays the hottest game in the whole snapshot. Winning the wrong trick remains the most reliable source of drama in the trick-taking family.
- **Partners and shared goals** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Potemkin Villages, Woo, Euchre, L'oaf, Moustache): Cooperation shows up in every family: full co-op in the Fellowship game, fixed pairs in Euchre and Woo, a shared quota in L'oaf, shifting teams in Moustache. Playing with someone is a draw in its own right, separate from the mechanism underneath.
- **Hands that refill, return or travel** (Pipoca, Crisps!, Bridge City Poker, LOOP): Discards come back when you pass in Pipoca, you draw after a pass in Crisps!, hands grow in Bridge City Poker and rotate between players in LOOP. A hand that changes during the round softens the luck of the deal and rewards planning more than one play ahead.
- **Rank orders that move** (Dickory, Flamenco, Schadenfreude): Dickory's conveyor decides which rank is highest, Flamenco lets one card be beaten by two that add up to half of it, and Schadenfreude gives the trick to the second-highest. Changing what 'higher' means resets everyone's habits with a single sentence.
- **Taken cards build something** (YRO, Trailblazers, Death Valley, Minikin City): A three-by-three guild, trail loops, a road-trip journey, a small city: what you take is placed and stays in view. It gives a quick card game a visible result and a second question beyond 'did I get it'.
- **Sets that can hurt** (Schadenfreude, Don't Go In There, Port Royal): Duplicates destroy each other in Schadenfreude, a completed set of cursed items is discarded in Don't Go In There, and a second ship of one colour ends the turn in Port Royal. Collecting stops being simply good, so every extra card is a judgement.

## Top themes

- **Animals** (Woo, Haunted Mouse, Honeypot, Moustache, Trailblazers): Birds of paradise, church mice, secret-agent bears: animal casts appear in all three families and are the easiest way to make a rules-light game look inviting.
- **Horror and comic doom** (Haunted Mouse, Don't Go In There, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Apocalypse, Schadenfreude): A haunted setting fits any game where something bad is about to happen to someone, and it is as natural for a bust in push-your-luck as for an unwanted trick.
- **Sea, road and trail** (Port Royal, Port Royal: Big Box, Death Valley, Trailblazers): Journeys suit push-your-luck: going further is the temptation and getting home is the bank. Port Royal is the most widely owned game in the set after the Tolkien licence.
- **Food and the workplace** (Crisps!, Pipoca, L'oaf): Crisps, popcorn and a bakery of shirkers: small, everyday, funny subjects that suit small boxes and need no explaining.
- **Fantasy and literature** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Dracula vs Van Helsing, YRO): A known story still carries the biggest numbers in the set: the Fellowship game logged 998 plays this week and is owned by over thirty thousand.
- **Dance and performance** (Flamenco, Woo): A dance step that splits one card into two, a courtship display built by two partners: performance themes explain combination play as showing off, which is what climbing games feel like.

## Top differentiators

- **Second-highest wins** (Schadenfreude): Four snapshots running at the top on one altered rule about who takes the trick. It is the benchmark for how little a design needs to change to feel new.
- **Pocket games for two** (Pipoca, Crisps!, Dickory, Death Valley, Dracula vs Van Helsing): Five of the twenty-four are built for one or two players, and Pipoca is eighteen cards that can be played standing up with no table. The two-player card game is a category of its own and climbing games adapt to it far more easily than trick-takers.
- **Partners build one play** (Woo): In Woo both partners contribute cards to a single meld. It makes partnership a matter of what you put on the table together, with no bidding conventions to learn.
- **A beaten card comes back** (Pipoca, Crisps!): Upgrading a card that was beaten, or drawing after a pass, pays the player who lost the exchange. It keeps both players in a two-player game alive to the end.
- **Splitting a number** (Flamenco): Beating an even card with two cards that add up to half of it gives low cards a way to win and adds arithmetic a family can do aloud.
- **Burn a suit, lowest score wins** (Bridge City Poker): Discarding one whole suit at the end of a round gives players a late decision that can rescue a bad hand, and ties the theme to the scoring.
- **I stack, you choose how deep** (Honeypot): One player arranges a pile and another decides how far into it to go. It joins cut-and-choose to push-your-luck and makes every pile a message from its maker.
- **Doing the least without sinking the group** (L'oaf): Everyone must jointly meet a quota while individually contributing as little as possible. The shirker's dilemma is instantly understood and produces accusation and laughter with almost no rules.
- **Everyone plays every hand** (LOOP): Hands pass from player to player so that all are played by everyone, which removes the complaint that the deal decided the game.
- **A classic back in play** (Euchre): A game from 1848 enters the source list with 84 logged plays in the week. Five-card hands, a turned-up trump and a race to three tricks are a reminder that short rounds and a tiny decision set still hold a table.
- **Cards that double as coins** (Port Royal, Port Royal: Big Box): Every card back is a coin, so the deck is also the bank. It removes components and makes each draw a small economic choice.

### 1. Don't Wake the Dog — 71.7/100

*Steal bones from your neighbour's hand one at a time. One of those cards is the dog.*

- **Family:** Push Your Luck
- **Core twist:** Each player holds six cards: five bones of different values and a dog. On your turn, pick an opponent and draw cards from their fanned hand one at a time, as many as you dare. Bones are yours when you stop. Draw the dog and everything goes back, with a point to its owner. The owner may rearrange the fan after every draw.
- **Theme:** A sleeping guard dog
- **Remixes:** Honeypot, Haunted Mouse, I stack, you choose how deep, Animals
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 5, learning feedback 6, mechanics 8, dynamics 8, aesthetics 9, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Drawing one at a time from a rival's fanned hand of five bones and a dog, with the owner reshuffling the fan between draws, is Old Maid turned into a face-to-face bluffing duel anyone can play, though the skill is mostly reading people and cards changing hands needs a tidy reset.

Don't Wake the Dog gives every player the same six cards: bones worth one to five and a sleeping dog. You hold them fanned, faces toward you. On your turn you pick an opponent and draw from their fan, one card at a time, laying each face-up in front of you. Stop whenever you like and the bones go into your score pile. Turn over the dog and it all goes back, and the owner scores a point for the scare. After every single draw the owner may rearrange their fan under the table. A round ends when everyone has been raided once, hands are reset from spare sets, and three rounds make a game. The decision is plain and repeated: one more card, from this person, who has just moved something to the left edge.

There is no deck to count, so what players learn is each other. Does this opponent tuck the dog in the middle or leave it at the end, do they reshuffle after every draw or only when you are close, do they push a card forward to bait you? Because the odds are stated by the cards remaining, one in six, then one in five, the mathematics is clear to a child, and the skill sits entirely in the bluff layered on top of it. Players also learn whom to raid: a fan that has already lost three bones has a one-in-three dog. The table watches every draw, and the feeling is the held breath of a playground game, with the owner's face doing half the work.

It is a game most people already half know, which is its reach: no reading, a dog on the cover, two minutes to teach, and it works from 2 to 4, with two players raiding each other twice a round. The scorer's risks are that it is mostly people-reading and that cards changing hands is fiddly. For the first, I would test bones of two colours with a bonus for a matched pair, so that which card you want matters as well as how many. For the second, giving each player a coloured back solves the reset. The playtest measure is the average number of cards drawn per raid; if it settles at two every time, the dog's penalty is too heavy.

### 2. Three Doors — 71.1/100

*Three piles, three colours of door. The backs tell you how dangerous each one is.*

- **Family:** Push Your Luck
- **Core twist:** The deck is split by card back into three piles: green doors hide small treasures and few ghosts, blue more of both, red the big treasures and the most ghosts. On each flip you choose which door to open. Three ghosts in your row and you lose it. Everyone can count what has already come out of each door.
- **Theme:** A haunted house
- **Remixes:** Don't Go In There, Honeypot, Flip for more or stop, Horror and comic doom
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 5, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Three piles whose card backs advertise their risk let players choose their own danger and count what has come out, a clear and learnable odds game with a family-friendly haunted house, though interaction is thin and counters may come to dominate.

Three Doors splits one deck into three face-down piles by the colour printed on the backs. A reference card on the table says what each door holds: behind green, treasures worth one or two and one card in six a ghost; behind blue, treasures of two to four and one in four a ghost; behind red, treasures of four to seven and one in three. On your turn you open doors one at a time, choosing the colour for each flip and adding the card to your row. Stop and you keep the treasure. A third ghost in your row and you lose all of it. Used cards go to open discard stacks beside their doors, so everyone can see what has already come out. The decision is two-layered: whether to go on, and through which door.

That second layer is what there is to learn. A first-time player treats red as brave and green as safe. After a few rounds they notice that green has had no ghost in twelve cards and is now the most dangerous pile on the table, or that red has given up five ghosts and is nearly clean. The game teaches conditional odds through plain observation, with the discard stacks as the evidence, and it does so without anyone doing arithmetic aloud. Good play is about order as well: take your risks while you have no ghosts, and move to the door that is statistically spent once you have two. The feeling is exploration with a rising pulse, and other players lean in because the piles belong to everyone.

A haunted house, a single deck with three back colours and one reference card make a very cheap box, and it plays 2 to 4 with no changes. The scorer points to thin interaction and to card counters. I would add one interactive rule and test it: when you bank, you may move the top card of any door, unseen, onto another, which muddies the count and gives the next player something to think about. I would also test reshuffling each door when its pile is half gone. The measure is whether an attentive newcomer beats a careless veteran by their third game.

### 3. Go On, Then — 69.7/100

*Any opponent can put a coin on the deck and dare you to flip again.*

- **Family:** Push Your Luck
- **Core twist:** While you are deciding whether to stop, any opponent may place a coin on the deck as a dare. If you flip again and survive, you take the coins. If you bust, each player who dared you takes a card from your row. Coins are points, so a dare is a real offer.
- **Theme:** Schoolyard dares
- **Remixes:** L'oaf, Port Royal, Cards that double as coins, Risk that other players set for you
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 5, learning feedback 7, mechanics 8, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Opponents paying a coin to dare you into one more flip turns the table's goading into a priced offer and gives every turn an audience, but free-for-all dare timing can get noisy and coin flow may favour whoever is dared most.

Go On, Then is a flip-and-bank game with a price on peer pressure. Everyone starts with five coins, which are points. On your turn you flip cards from a shared deck into a row: goods worth one to four, and hazards in three kinds, where a second hazard of the same kind is a bust. After any flip you may bank. But before you decide, each opponent may put one coin on top of the deck as a dare. If you flip again and survive, the coins on the deck are yours on top of the card. If you bust, each player who dared takes one card of their choice from your row and gets their coin back, and you get nothing. With no dares, a bust simply discards the row.

The pattern to learn is that a dare is a bet with odds everyone can work out. Daring a player who has one hazard showing and four good cards down costs a coin if they survive and pays a card worth up to four if they do not. Players learn to dare when the row is rich and the hazard count is live, and to recognise when a rival's dare is a signal that they have counted the deck. The active player learns that coins on the deck change the sum: a flip they would never take for one more card may be worth it for three coins. It turns the heckling that happens around any push-your-luck game into a mechanism, and the feeling is loud and friendly, with every turn belonging to the whole table.

The title is something people already say, the components are a deck and a bag of coins, and it suits 3 to 4 players in ten to fifteen minutes. The scorer's risks are noisy timing and coin flow toward whoever is dared most. I would fix timing by having dares placed in turn order, once round, after each flip. For the flow, I would test capping the deck at two coins and paying a successful banker one coin from the bank for each dare they declined. The measure is whether the richest player after round one wins more than half the time.

### 4. Breaking Point — 69.3/100

*There is no trump until someone can't follow suit. Then there is, for good.*

- **Family:** Trick-taking
- **Core twist:** The round starts with no trump. The first card played off-suit loses that trick as usual, but its suit becomes trump for the rest of the round. The first player to run out of a suit chooses trump by what they throw away.
- **Theme:** A dam about to burst
- **Remixes:** Euchre, Apocalypse, A classic back in play, Rank orders that move
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 8, mechanics 9, dynamics 6, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Trump fixed by the first off-suit discard is one sentence, needs no bidding and makes voids a visible weapon everyone can see coming, though the idea has traditional precedents and the first void is often an accident of the deal rather than a decision.

Breaking Point is trick-taking with the bidding taken out and the drama of trump left in. Deal nine cards each from four suits; nothing is turned up and nobody calls anything. Play is must-follow. The first time in the round that a player cannot follow suit, whatever they throw away loses that trick as usual, and its suit becomes trump from the next trick until the end of the round. A marker is set on that suit. Tricks are a point each. The central decision belongs to whoever is nearest to running out of something: getting void first is the power to choose trump, and what you discard at that moment decides the next six tricks.

Players learn to see the round in two halves. Before the break, everyone is racing to empty a short suit and trying not to help anyone else do it, so leading your singleton is strong and leading a suit an opponent has shown short in is reckless. After it, the round is an ordinary trump game in which one player chose trump to suit their own hand. The skill is in preparing for both: shed your short suit, but keep length in the suit you mean to nominate. Every hand makes the point visibly, because the marker lands on the table at a moment everyone watched arrive. It gives newcomers the concept of trump as an event with a cause, which is easier to grasp than a suit named in advance.

It needs only a deck and a marker, the rule is one sentence, and it plays 2 to 4, with two players each using a face-up row of four extra cards. The scorer notes precedents in traditional games and the risk that the first void is dealt, not earned. I would playtest passing two cards before play, which lets everyone choose their short suit, and a version in which the breaker scores one point less for the round as the price of the privilege. The measure is how often the player who sets trump also wins the round; much above forty percent at four players and the privilege is too strong.

### 5. Snowdrift — 68.8/100

*Every pass adds a snowball to the drift. The sixth one buries you.*

- **Family:** Hybrid
- **Core twist:** Climbing with a shared bust. Each time anyone passes, a snowball token goes on the drift. The player whose pass adds the sixth must pick up the whole discard pile from the current round into their hand, and the drift is cleared. Everyone can see how deep it is before they decide to pass.
- **Theme:** A snowed-in mountain hut
- **Remixes:** Apocalypse, Haunted Mouse, L'oaf, Shed your hand by beating the last play, Doing the least without sinking the group
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 8, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** A shared drift where the sixth pass buries its maker under the round's discards makes passing a public countdown and forces risky plays to dodge it, though with two players the count is too predictable and a large pickup can knock someone out of contention.

Snowdrift is a climbing game in which passing fills a shared hazard. Cards run one to ten in four suits and players are dealt ten each. Lead a single, a set or a run; each player in turn beats it with a higher play of the same shape or passes. Every pass puts a snowball token on the drift in the middle of the table. The player whose pass is the sixth is buried: they pick up every card played in the current round and add them to their hand, and the drift is cleared. Rounds end as usual when all pass in succession, and the first player to empty their hand wins. The decision at five snowballs is sharp: beat the table with something you wanted to keep, or pass and take the pile.

The new pattern is counting passes as carefully as cards. Players learn that a pass early in the count is cheap and a pass at four is an attack on the next player, that leading a high play which forces three passes in a row moves the drift three steps at once, and that the worst time to be buried is late in a long round when the pile is thick. They also discover that a buried hand is not always a ruined one, since it now holds the round's runs and sets, and the pick-up becomes a calculated loan in the right hands. All of this is visible in six tokens. The feeling is a shared wince as the count climbs, and the theme makes the rule self-explanatory.

Shedding games are familiar from childhood in most countries, and this adds one prop and one sentence. It is listed for 2 to 4, and the scorer is right that two is weak, because the count simply alternates. I would playtest a limit of four snowballs at two players with a neutral token added after every round, and at all counts cap a burial at the ten most recent cards so that nobody is knocked out of contention. The measure is whether a buried player still wins about one hand in five; lower than that and the penalty needs softening.

### 6. Heavy Is the Hand — 68.0/100

*When someone goes out, you pay for the single biggest card you are still holding.*

- **Family:** Ladder Climbing
- **Core twist:** The round ends when one player empties their hand. Everyone else takes penalty points equal to the rank of the highest card left in their hand, however many cards they hold. High cards win rounds and are the worst thing to be caught with, so you must judge when to let them go.
- **Theme:** A king's heavy crown
- **Remixes:** Bridge City Poker, Schadenfreude, Burn a suit, lowest score wins, Sets that can hurt
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 9, dynamics 6, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Penalising only the single highest card you are caught holding is a one-line scoring rule that makes power cards a liability and is perfectly legible when it bites, though it is a scoring tweak on a standard chassis and may reward simply dumping high cards early.

Heavy Is the Hand is a standard climbing game with a different bill at the end. Ranks run one to twelve in four suits, ten cards each. Play singles, sets and runs, beat or pass, and the round stops the moment one player is out. Everyone else then takes penalty points equal to the rank of the single highest card still in their hand, and nothing else: holding eight cards topped by a 5 costs five, and holding one card that happens to be a 12 costs twelve. Lowest total after four rounds wins. The decision this creates is about your best cards. They are what win rounds and let you lead your awkward combinations, and they are also the entire cost of being caught.

Players learn a new sense of timing. In an ordinary shedding game you hoard your top cards for the finish; here the finish is when they are most dangerous. The first lesson is to cash a 12 while a rival still has six cards. The second is to watch hand sizes as a clock: when someone is down to three, every high card on the table is a liability and the correct play may be to spend your ace on a trick you would normally let go. The third is that a hand cleared of high cards can sit back and lose cheaply, which is a legitimate plan. Because the penalty is one visible number, everyone understands at once why they were hurt. The feeling is of a hot object being passed quickly as the end approaches.

It is a deck of cards and one rule about scoring, so it costs nothing to make and teaches in a minute to anyone who has played a shedding game, at 2 to 4 players. The scorer's doubt is that it is a tweak and may reward dumping high cards immediately. I would test paying two points to whoever goes out, so that keeping the cards that win rounds has a matching reward, and letting a set of three or more high cards be played only as a lead. The measure is the average round in which the top rank is played; if it is always the first, the balance is wrong.

### 7. Leftover Slice — 67.5/100

*Pass, and you take the smallest card on the table home with you.*

- **Family:** Ladder Climbing
- **Core twist:** A player who passes must pick up the lowest card of the play currently on top and add it to their hand. Each play can be raided once per card, so a pair of 2s led into three opponents hands out two of them. Passing is never free.
- **Theme:** The last slices of pizza
- **Remixes:** Pipoca, Crisps!, A beaten card comes back, Food and the workplace
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Making every pass pick up the lowest card of the top play fixes climbing's dead turns with one rule and a relatable pizza theme, but leading junk to feed opponents could dominate and stretch rounds if not tuned.

Leftover Slice changes what a pass costs. It is a climbing game with cards from one to ten in four suits, nine dealt to each player. A lead may be a single, a set or a run, and each player in turn beats it or passes. A player who passes must take the lowest card of the play currently on top of the pile into their hand; that card is then gone from the pile, so a pair that is raided twice has been handed out entirely, and later passers take nothing. The round ends when everyone has passed in succession and the last player to play leads. First to empty their hand wins. The decision moves to the leader: what you lead is what your opponents will be made to eat.

Players quickly learn two uses for the rule. Leading low rubbish is an attack, since anyone who cannot or will not beat a pair of 2s takes one home. But a card picked up is also a card that might complete a run, so players learn to pass on purpose when the lowest card on the table is one they need, and leaders learn to check what a pass would give away. The usual dead turn of a shedding game, where you pass and wait, now changes your hand every time. Hand sizes rise and fall in full view, and the dynamic is a constant small exchange of unwanted cards. The feeling is the mild comedy of being left with the last slice nobody wanted.

Pizza is a theme that needs no translation, the box is a deck, and it plays 2 to 4 with no adjustment. The scorer's risk is that feeding junk to opponents dominates and rounds drag. I would playtest allowing a passer to refuse the card by discarding one of equal or higher rank, and ending the hand when the deck of spare cards, one dealt aside per round, runs out, with the smallest hand winning. The measure is average hand length in turns; a nine-card hand should finish in about twelve to fifteen rounds of the table, and if it takes twenty the pick-up is too strong.

### 8. Gavel — 65.8/100

*Six lots, twelve cards. Every card you bid is gone, whether you win or not.*

- **Family:** Ladder Climbing
- **Core twist:** Six prize cards are sold one at a time. Bidding is climbing: beat the last play with a higher single, set or run, or pass. The last player to play takes the lot, and every card played by anyone is discarded. Nobody draws, so twelve cards must cover all six auctions.
- **Theme:** A country auction
- **Remixes:** Port Royal, Bridge City Poker, Cards that double as coins, Shed your hand by beating the last play
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.7
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 6
- **Scorer's note:** Using climbing plays as all-pay bids across six lots with no draws makes every card a budget decision and gives the genre a clear objective, but it still assumes players know climbing combinations and the last lots can be foregone conclusions.

Gavel uses the plays of a climbing game as bids. Six lot cards are laid out in a row, worth from two to seven points, with the order visible from the start. Each player is dealt twelve cards and there are no draws. For each lot, the opening bidder leads a single, a set or a run; others in turn beat it with a higher play of the same shape or pass and are out of that lot. The last player to play takes the lot and opens the next. Every card played by anyone, winner or not, goes to the discard. After six lots, highest total wins. The decision is how much of a fixed hand to spend on each prize, knowing that losing bids are paid for too.

The pattern is an all-pay auction in which the currency has structure. A run of four is a strong bid that cannot be split later; three single high cards can contest three different lots. Players learn to bleed opponents on lots they do not want with one cheap raise, to recognise when the opener's shape suits their hand, and to save a pair for the seven-point lot at the end because by then few players have combinations left. Since all bids are public, the discard tells everyone who is nearly spent. The dynamic is a series of short, tense auctions with visible budgets, and the feeling is the satisfaction of buying something for exactly one card more than the next person could afford.

Auctions are understood by everyone, and the lot row gives the game a goal that pure shedding lacks. It needs a deck and six lot cards and plays 2 to 4. The scorer's concerns are that it assumes knowledge of climbing combinations and that late lots can be foregone conclusions. I would test a beginner mode with only singles and pairs, and dealing the lots face-down after the third so that nobody can plan the endgame exactly. I would also try letting a player who passes on a lot draw one card. The measure is how often the final lot is contested by at least two players.

### 9. Last to Blink — 65.8/100

*Passing is free, unless you were the last one to give in.*

- **Family:** Hybrid
- **Core twist:** Climbing as a game of chicken. When a round ends, the player who passed last, the one who held out longest against the winner, takes a penalty card. Players who dropped out early pay nothing. Each time you beat the table you are betting that someone else will fold after you.
- **Theme:** A staring contest
- **Remixes:** L'oaf, Haunted Mouse, Doing the least without sinking the group, Shed your hand by beating the last play
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 6, mechanics 8, dynamics 8, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 6
- **Scorer's note:** Penalising only the last player to pass recasts every beat as a game of chicken using a single rule, creating real stare-downs, but it needs three or more and with early folding free the table may collapse into timid rounds.

Last to Blink is a climbing game where the question is not whether to pass but when. Deal ten cards each from a deck of ones to tens. Lead any single, set or run; in turn each player beats it or passes, and a player who has passed is out of that round. When the round ends, the player who makes the final play leads next as usual, and the player who was the last to pass, the one who stayed in longest and then gave way, takes a penalty card worth minus two. Everyone who dropped out earlier pays nothing. Going out first scores five, and a hand of three rounds makes a game. Every time you beat the table you are staking two points that someone after you will stay in and then fold.

What players learn is to judge commitment. Beating a play early in a round is safe when two players are still to act, since one of them is likely to continue and then lose. Beating it when only you and the leader remain is a duel that one of you will lose, and the hand strengths decide who. Players learn to drop out the moment they sense two strong hands above them, to stay in with nothing if the player behind them looks eager, and to use a modest beat to drag an opponent into a round they cannot win. The penalty cards pile up in front of the stubborn, so the lesson is visible. The dynamic is a string of small stare-downs, and the feeling is the nervous fun of a game of chicken.

One rule added to a familiar game, a deck of cards and a dozen penalty markers: it is cheap, quick and easy to explain, for 3 to 4 players. The scorer's risk is that free early folding makes everyone timid. I would playtest paying the round's winner a point for each player who contested it, so that drawing people in is rewarded, and giving the penalty only when at least two players beat the lead. The measure is the average number of plays per round; if it falls below two, nobody is fighting and the incentive to stay needs raising.

### 10. Favours — 64.8/100

*You have two boosts to give, and you can't use them on yourself.*

- **Family:** Trick-taking
- **Core twist:** Each player has two favour tokens per round. As another player plays a card, you may place a favour on it to raise it three ranks. If that card wins the trick, you score a point along with its owner. Favours cannot be placed on your own cards.
- **Theme:** Village gossip and good turns
- **Remixes:** Moustache, L'oaf, Partners and shared goals, Risk that other players set for you
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 6, mechanics 7, dynamics 8, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 6
- **Scorer's note:** Two plus-three boosts that can only go on someone else's card and pay you if it wins create shifting, wordless alliances inside ordinary trick play, but kingmaking arguments and timing disputes over when a favour may be placed are real risks.

Favours is trick-taking in which your most useful resource can only help somebody else. Hands are nine cards, must-follow, with one trump suit, and tricks are a point each. Every player has two favour tokens per round. When another player plays a card, and before the next card is played, you may put a favour on it: that card counts three ranks higher. If it goes on to win the trick, its owner scores the trick and you score a point as well. You may never favour your own card, and one card can carry only one favour. The decision is whom to back and when, and for the player being backed, whether the help is welcome.

Players learn that a favour is a small alliance with a motive behind it. The obvious use is to back a winner for a shared point. The better use is to back the player who is second to act so that the leader on the scoreboard loses a trick they were counting on, or to lift a card that makes its owner win a trick they were trying to duck. Favours placed by the last player to act are nearly certain; favours placed early are speculative and cheaper to be wrong about. Because tokens are public, the table knows who can still intervene. It makes every trick a brief negotiation conducted without words, and the feeling is fellowship of a sly kind: you are always helping someone, for your own reasons.

The idea is told in a sentence, eight wooden tokens are the only addition to a deck, and it plays 3 to 4. The scorer names two real risks. For kingmaking, I would test letting each player receive at most one favour per trick and scoring the favour-giver only if they did not also win a trick that round with their own card, which keeps favours a tool for the trailing players. For timing disputes, favours could be declared only by the next player in turn as they play. The measure is whether the round's top scorer received more favours than average; they should not.
