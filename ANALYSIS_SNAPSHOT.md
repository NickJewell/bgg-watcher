# Trick-taking design analysis — 2026-11-11

Generated by a scheduled Claude Code session from the 20 most active games in the 2026-11-11 snapshot. 100 variants were generated and scored; the top 10 are pitched below. Scores are the model's judgement against a fixed rubric, useful for sorting ideas, not a forecast.

**Source games:** Schadenfreude, Apocalypse, Potemkin Villages, Minikin City, Moustache, Trick to the Future, Can't Win, Zombie Princess, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Fishing 2, Tax the Rich, Trick Stakes, Oshaka Trick, Mythical Dice, The Six of VIII, Matchmakers, Cry Baby, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Off With Their Heads!, Shards of Creation

## Top mechanics

- **Exact-bid prediction** (Can't Win, Cry Baby, Mythical Dice, Tax the Rich): Bidding turns a dealt hand into a self-set puzzle, so a weak hand is as playable as a strong one. The current games do not stop at 'name a number': they bolt a second commitment onto the bid (a constraint in Can't Win, a hand orientation in Cry Baby, a trump call in Tax the Rich), which gives players a new pattern to read every deal.
- **Winning is dangerous: avoidance, bust and restraint scoring** (Schadenfreude, Apocalypse, Oshaka Trick, Zombie Princess): When a trick can hurt, every card is a question rather than a reflex, and the table gets the laughter of watching someone be forced to take what they did not want. It is the cheapest way to make low cards matter and it keeps a trailing player in the game.
- **Cooperative trick-taking with personal goals** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Potemkin Villages): The Fellowship game has by far the largest play count and ownership in the set. Giving each player a small, different job inside a shared trick-taking hand produces fellowship without a quarterback, because nobody can see the cards needed to boss the others.
- **Shifting or temporary partnerships** (Moustache, Tax the Rich, Zombie Princess, The Six of VIII, Matchmakers): Partnerships that change each round give the warmth of team play without needing a fixed foursome, and they stop a runaway leader because today's ally is tomorrow's opponent.
- **Tricks that feed a second system** (Minikin City, Fishing 2, Trick Stakes, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Off With Their Heads!): The trick becomes the engine rather than the score: it sets draft order, moves a horse, fills a sheet or stocks next round's hand. That gives non-card-players something concrete to look at and makes finishing second or last in a trick a real choice.
- **Set collection from captured cards** (Schadenfreude, Matchmakers, Shards of Creation, Fishing 2): Which cards are in the trick matters more than how many tricks you take, so discards from players who cannot follow become gifts or poison. It rewards watching the whole table, not just the led suit.
- **Variable powers and asymmetric suits** (Potemkin Villages, Moustache, Zombie Princess, Shards of Creation, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game): A character or a suit with its own rule gives each player an identity and a reason to replay, and it is the natural hook for a licence. The cost is teach time, so the successful ones keep each power to a single line.
- **Changing a card's value at the moment of play** (Trick to the Future, Cry Baby): A spendable resource or a second face on the card softens the tyranny of the deal and adds a visible bluff: opponents can count your fuel or see half your options, so reading people starts to matter as much as counting cards.

## Top themes

- **Fantasy worlds and big licences** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Shards of Creation, Zombie Princess, Matchmakers, Off With Their Heads!): A known world carries a small card game into mainstream shops, and characters map cleanly onto roles and suits. The lesson for an unlicensed design is to pick a setting the buyer already understands from the cover.
- **Doom, horror and gallows humour** (Apocalypse, Zombie Princess, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Oshaka Trick, Schadenfreude): Dark-but-funny framing suits games where winning hurts: the theme tells players in advance that misfortune is the entertainment, which makes a punishing rule feel like a joke rather than an injustice.
- **Courts, class and history** (The Six of VIII, Tax the Rich, Potemkin Villages, Cry Baby, Off With Their Heads!): Rank is what playing cards have always depicted, so hierarchy themes explain card strength for free, and upending the hierarchy (revolution, a new queen, a fake village) explains the twist.
- **Animals and gentle nature** (Moustache, Fishing 2, Matchmakers): Friendly animal art is the lowest-friction route to a family table and softens competitive or chaotic rules. It is under-used in this set relative to how well it sells.
- **Races, wagers and spectacle** (Trick Stakes, Mythical Dice, Trick to the Future): Framing the hand as a race or a bet gives spectators something to cheer and gives the score a physical shape on the table, which helps people who do not naturally count tricks.
- **Small-scale building** (Minikin City, Potemkin Villages): Leaving something built in front of each player at the end gives a sense of authorship that a pile of captured tricks does not, and it photographs well.

## Top differentiators

- **The trick is not won by the highest card** (Schadenfreude, Off With Their Heads!): Schadenfreude is the hottest game in the snapshot on a single rule change: second-highest wins. Changing who wins the trick is a one-sentence teach that breaks every habit an experienced player has, so novices and veterans start level.
- **A self-chosen handicap before play** (Can't Win, Cry Baby): Letting players pick their own restriction after seeing their hand converts luck of the deal into a decision, and creates a story ('I chose this') when it goes wrong.
- **A shared doom clock in the middle of the table** (Apocalypse, Potemkin Villages): Tokens that everyone flips and nobody wants to finish give a competitive game a common enemy and a visible timer, producing brinkmanship without any direct attack.
- **The hand or the winnings carry over** (Trick Stakes, Fishing 2): Keeping the same hand, or playing next round with what you caught, lets players learn their cards the way they learn a character. It is Koster's mastery loop made physical: the pattern is literally in your hand again.
- **Rules or trumps that move on a visible schedule** (Moustache, The Six of VIII, Shards of Creation, Zombie Princess): A timeline or a new rule each round refreshes the pattern before it is solved, and because the change is announced in advance it rewards planning rather than punishing with surprise.
- **A hard score ceiling** (Schadenfreude, Trick to the Future): A target you must not overshoot turns the leader into the most endangered player and gives every late trick two meanings. It is a built-in catch-up rule that needs no extra text.
- **A spendable resource beside the hand** (Trick to the Future, Minikin City): A few tokens that bend a card give agency to a bad hand and a public tell to read. The risk is fiddliness, so the successful versions keep it to one kind of token with one effect.
- **Built to work at two** (Dracula vs Van Helsing, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Minikin City, Potemkin Villages, Matchmakers): Trick-taking traditionally needs three or four, yet couples are the largest buying group. Designs that are honest two-player games rather than a dummy-hand variant reach a market the classics cannot.
- **Something on the table that is not a card** (Mythical Dice, Trick Stakes, Off With Their Heads!, Apocalypse): Dice, a race track, a score sheet or flipping tokens give the game a silhouette on a shelf and a toy factor at the table, as long as the cost stays within a small box.

### 1. Five-Alarm — 77.3/100

*Add chilli to any card. Whoever wins the trick gets the chillies.*

- **Core twist:** Everyone starts with three chilli tokens. When you play a card you may put chillies on it, each adding three to its rank. All chillies spent in a trick go to the trick's winner. Spending heavily to win gets your tokens back plus everyone else's; spending and losing arms an opponent.
- **Theme:** A chilli cook-off
- **Remixes:** Trick to the Future, Cry Baby, A spendable resource beside the hand, Changing a card's value at the moment of play
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 8, mechanics 9, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Chillies that add three to a card and all go to the trick winner is a one-line betting rule producing bluffs, escalation and a visible swing of power, with the risk that an early chilli lead snowballs if no catch-up exists.

Five-Alarm is a plain must-follow trick-taker with one trump suit and cards ranked 1 to 12, plus three wooden chillies in front of each player. A round is eight tricks. When you play a card you may stack any number of your chillies on it, each adding three to its rank, and every chilli in the trick goes to whoever wins it. The central decision is on almost every card: is this trick worth heating up, and if I heat it, can the player after me afford to go hotter? Because chillies are public, you can count exactly how far each opponent can stretch, and the last seat in a trick has a real, visible advantage that makes winning the lead a mixed blessing. Tricks score one point; chillies are worth nothing at the end, so they must be spent.

What players learn is tempo. First plays teach the obvious lesson, that spending and losing hands your opponent a weapon. Over repeated plays the subtler patterns appear: spending one chilli early to make a middling card look committed, leading a suit where the rich player is void so their chillies are useless, bleeding a hoarder by forcing them to overspend on a trick you never wanted. The pattern stays alive because the right play depends on the token count around the table, which is different every hand. As dynamics this is a betting game folded inside a trick: escalation, bluffs, groans when a seven with two chillies beats an ace. The feeling is cheerful brinkmanship, and the theme explains the rule without a word of rules text.

Reach comes from the toy factor and a ten-second teach to anyone who has played Hearts or Spades, at the cost of a deck and a dozen wooden bits. It plays 2 to 4; at two, deal a three-card face-up row per player to add information. The biggest risk is the one the scorer named: whoever wins the first hot trick holds six or more chillies and may snowball. I would playtest three brakes in order of simplicity: a cap of two chillies per card, chillies returning to an even three each round, and the player with the fewest chillies leading. Track how often the first-trick winner wins the round; if it is much above one in four at four players, the brake is not strong enough.

### 2. Too Hot to Hold — 75.8/100

*Three tricks is a feast. Four and you're burned.*

- **Core twist:** Each trick is worth a point, but the instant any player takes their fourth trick the round stops and that player scores zero while everyone else banks what they hold. Players sitting on three are suddenly everyone's target, and every lead is a hot potato.
- **Theme:** Baked potatoes round a campfire
- **Remixes:** Schadenfreude, Oshaka Trick, A hard score ceiling, Winning is dangerous: avoidance, bust and restraint scoring
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 10, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** A fourth trick busts you and ends the round is one sentence that makes anyone on three a table-wide target and every lead a hot potato, with the main risk that it is a small twist on plain tricks and needs three or more players.

Too Hot to Hold deals ten cards each from a four-suit deck with no trump and plays ordinary must-follow tricks worth a point apiece. The whole game is one sentence: the moment anyone takes a fourth trick, the round ends, that player scores nothing, and everyone else banks what they have. With ten tricks available and four players, somebody is nearly always pushed to the edge, so the central decision arrives when you sit on two: take a third and become the table's target, or stay small and hope someone else burns first. Once a player holds three, every lead is aimed at them, with low cards in their long suits and suits they are known to be short in, and their job is to duck for the rest of the round.

The learning curve is gentler than it looks and longer than it looks. New players learn in one round that high cards are dangerous late. Then they learn that three tricks early is a trap but three tricks late is the winning position, that keeping a low card in every suit is life insurance, and that ending the round by burning an opponent is only worth it when your own bank is ahead of the others. That last point keeps it from being solved: the two players who are not on three do not want the same thing, and the one in the lead may prefer the round to run on. The dynamics are loud and easy to follow, with temporary alliances that form by eye contact, and the feeling is the shared glee of a hot potato, where the victim usually laughs first.

This is a deck of cards and nothing else, a rule that fits on the box, and a theme a child understands, which is exactly the Schadenfreude recipe aimed at a broader table. It is designed for 3 to 4 players and I would resist stretching it. The main risk is that it reads as a thin twist: experienced players may feel a round is decided by the deal. I would test whether a pass of two cards before play, and a game length of first to fifteen, give enough control, and I would record how often the burned player could have avoided it with different play. If that is under half, the hands need to be longer or the threshold needs to scale with player count.

### 3. Trick or Treat — 75.2/100

*Only the leader knows whether the house at the end of this trick has sweets or a scare.*

- **Core twist:** Each trick is fought for a face-down house card. Before leading, the leader alone peeks at it: treats are worth one to three points, tricks cost one to two. The leader then leads any card they like, and everyone else must judge from the lead, and from the leader's face, whether this is a trick to win or to dodge.
- **Theme:** Halloween night on a suburban street
- **Remixes:** Trick Stakes, Oshaka Trick, Zombie Princess, Doom, horror and gallows humour, Winning is dangerous: avoidance, bust and restraint scoring
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 6, mechanics 9, dynamics 8, aesthetics 9, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Only the leader peeking at a house that may be a treat or a penalty turns every lead into a readable bluff with an ideal theme and one extra rule, with the risks that reading faces replaces card skill and the theme sells seasonally.

Trick or Treat is played over two hands of nine tricks. A deck of house cards sits face-down in the middle: most are treats worth one to three points, about a third are tricks costing one or two. Before each trick, the leader alone lifts the top house and looks. Then they lead any card, play follows suit in the normal way with one trump suit, and the winner of the trick takes the house face-up. The decision sits with the leader first: knowing the house is a three-point treat, do I lead my ace and announce it, or lead something modest and hope to sneak through? Then it passes to everyone else: the leader has just led a low club with a straight face, so is this a house to win or to dodge?

What players learn is each other. Early games are played on the cards, and a strong lead means a good house. Within a few rounds leaders start leading strongly into bad houses to stick someone with them, and the table learns to weigh the lead against the leader's position: a player who is behind needs points and is probably telling the truth, a player who is short in the suit they just led probably wants someone else to win. Because winning a trick makes you the next leader, taking a bad house also buys the next peek, and that trade is the part that keeps rewarding experience. The dynamics are a bluffing game that never stops the card play for a separate bluffing phase, and the feeling is light suspense with a reveal every thirty seconds.

The hook is on the cover, the components are two small decks, and it works from 2 to 4 because the bluff needs only one other person. The scorer is right about both risks. On skill, I would playtest with experienced trick-takers and check that the better card player still wins more often than not; if reading faces dominates, reduce the number of penalty houses, or show the house to the whole table on every third trick so that card play decides those. On seasonality, the same mechanism reskins cleanly as doors in a hotel corridor or parcels on a doorstep, and I would hold that option open for a publisher who wants a year-round box.

### 4. Called Shot — 74.8/100

*Don't say how many tricks you'll win. Say which ones.*

- **Core twist:** Eight tricks a round, numbered on a shared rack. Before play, each player puts chalk tokens on the numbers of the exact tricks they will win, in full view. You score two points for each called trick you win and lose one for each uncalled trick you win. Everyone can see who needs trick five.
- **Theme:** A neighbourhood pool hall
- **Remixes:** Can't Win, Exact-bid prediction, The Six of VIII
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.8
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 8, mastery depth 8, learning feedback 8, mechanics 7, dynamics 8, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Bidding which tricks rather than how many puts everyone's needs on public display, so the table can see exactly who must win trick five and fight over it, though predicting timing is a harder ask for novices than predicting a count.

Called Shot gives each player eight cards and puts a rack numbered one to eight in the middle of the table. After looking at their hands, players take turns placing chalk tokens on the numbers of the tricks they intend to win, as many or as few as they like, and several players may call the same trick. Then the hand is played, must-follow with a trump suit turned from the deck. A called trick you win scores two; any trick you win without having called it costs one. The decision is front-loaded into the calling and then tested continuously: my king of spades will win a trick, but will spades have been led by trick three, or do I have to hold the lead from trick two to make sure of it?

The new pattern to learn is timing. A count bid asks how strong your hand is; this asks when your hand is strong, which is something even practised players have rarely been asked to plan. Players discover that calling consecutive tricks is far easier than calling scattered ones because the lead carries over, that the first trick is the only one whose leader is known, and that a contested number is where the game is decided. Because every call is public, the table can see that two people need trick five and one of them must fail, and the uninvolved players get to choose whom to help. That produces negotiation without any negotiation rule, and a satisfying, readable post-mortem: you can point at the rack and see exactly where a plan broke.

The box line is strong and the rack gives it a look on the table; chalk and a pool-hall setting are cheap, friendly and not tied to any age group. It supports 2 to 4, with two players calling from a hand of ten and playing eight. The risk is that novices find calling specific tricks opaque and simply guess. I would playtest a first round where players call only one trick each, growing to free calling in round three, and measure how often first-time players make at least half their calls by their second game. If they do not, a softer penalty for uncalled wins is the first thing to change.

### 5. Slow and Steady — 73.2/100

*You race a hare and a tortoise, and you score whichever is behind.*

- **Core twist:** Each player has two runners on a shared track. Winning a trick moves your hare three spaces. Playing the lowest card of the led suit moves your tortoise two. At the end, your score is the position of whichever of your two animals is further back. You need to win and to lose in roughly equal measure.
- **Theme:** Aesop's tortoise and hare
- **Remixes:** Trick Stakes, Off With Their Heads!, Races, wagers and spectacle, Tricks that feed a second system
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Scoring your trailing animal, with the hare fed by wins and the tortoise by lowest cards, makes both ends of the hand valuable and shows progress on a shared track through a fable everyone knows, though middle cards are dead and it needs three or more.

Slow and Steady puts a single race track on the table and gives each player a hare and a tortoise. Hands are ten cards, must-follow, one trump. After each trick the winner's hare jumps three spaces and whoever played the lowest card of the led suit moves their tortoise two. When the hand ends you score the position of whichever of your two animals is further back, and the game is the best of three hands. So the central decision on every card is which animal this card is for: a high card feeds the hare, a low card feeds the tortoise, and anything in the middle is a card you want to lose quietly on a trick where it achieves neither.

The lesson that unfolds over plays is balance and the ratio behind it. Three hare wins equal roughly four or five tortoise moves, so a hand of big cards is not a good hand, and a hand of small ones is not a bad one. Players then learn that the lowest card of the led suit is contested just as hard as the highest, that leading a suit where you hold the two is a tortoise play, and that a card thrown off-suit moves nothing at all. Feedback is immediate and physical: you can see your hare stranded ahead of your tortoise and know what your next five cards need to do. At the table it produces two fights in every trick, and the feeling is the pleasant surprise of a fable that turns out to be mechanically true.

Everyone knows the story, the track and eight wooden animals make it look like a family game rather than a card game, and the rule is two lines. It is a 3 to 4 player design. The scorer's concern about dead middle cards is the real risk: a hand full of sixes and sevens could feel helpless. I would test letting the second-highest card of the led suit nudge either animal one space, and separately a pass of three cards before play, and choose whichever leaves the fewest turns where a player says they had no choice. I would also check the 3-and-2 movement values across a hundred simulated hands so neither animal is systematically the bottleneck.

### 6. Tapas Night — 73.0/100

*Win the trick to order first, but everyone eats.*

- **Core twist:** Every card is both a rank for the trick and a dish for your table. After a trick, players choose in order of card strength, winner first, one card each from those just played and add it to their meal. Meals score for complete sets of starter, main and dessert and for repeated favourites. The card you play to win may be the dish someone else wants.
- **Theme:** A crowded tapas bar
- **Remixes:** Minikin City, Matchmakers, Tricks that feed a second system, Set collection from captured cards
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Everyone drafting a dish from the trick in strength order means no one sits a trick out and the card you win with may feed a rival, wrapped in a Sushi Go-friendly theme, though set scoring adds a second thing to teach.

In Tapas Night every card has a rank and suit for trick-taking and a dish for scoring: starters, mains and desserts in a handful of varieties. A trick is played in the normal must-follow way. Then, instead of the winner taking everything, the players pick one card each from the cards just played, in order of strength: the winner first, then the next-highest card of the led suit, down to off-suit cards last. Each card goes face-up into its new owner's meal. After eight tricks, a complete starter-main-dessert set scores five, and each repeat of your most common dish scores an extra point. The central decision is that the card you play is also a dish you are putting on the table for someone to take, quite possibly not you.

The pattern is richer than a draft and richer than a trick. Beginners play high to choose first. Then they notice that the strong cards are often the dull dishes, that coming second is nearly as good as winning when only one dish in the trick matters, and that playing the dessert an opponent needs is safe only if you can out-rank them. Because meals are face-up, what each rival is collecting is public, so reading the table is rewarded without memory work. The dynamics are constant, low-stakes interaction with nobody sitting a trick out, and the feeling is the convivial squabble of a shared meal, where you get something every time and sometimes exactly what you wanted.

Food, a draft-and-collect scoring model that millions already know from other small card games, a single deck, and a 2 to 4 range with no adjustments make this the most obviously shelf-ready design on the list. The risk is teaching two systems at once, and the related danger that the trick-taking becomes a formality in front of the set collection. I would playtest with a deliberately tiny scoring card of two rules, and watch whether players ever choose to lose a trick on purpose; if they never do, rank and dish are too well aligned, and the fix is to print the most wanted dishes on the weakest cards.

### 7. Leftovers — 72.0/100

*Your winning cards get eaten. Your losers are tomorrow's dinner.*

- **Core twist:** A card that wins a trick goes to your score pile for good. Every card you played that did not win returns to your hand for the next round, topped up with two fresh cards. Your hand gets weaker each time you succeed, and four rounds later the player who spent their aces early is playing with scraps.
- **Theme:** A week of fridge leftovers
- **Remixes:** Fishing 2, Trick Stakes, The hand or the winnings carry over
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.7
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 8, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 6, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Winning cards leave for good while losers come back as your next hand is a clean self-balancing rule that teaches when to spend an ace, though the leftovers theme is less inviting than the mechanism and late rounds may drag for the leader.

Leftovers is played over four rounds with a single trump suit. Each round is a normal must-follow hand of eight tricks. The change is in what happens to the cards: the card that wins a trick goes face-down into its owner's score pile and is worth a point, while every other card played is set aside by its owner and picked up again at the end of the round. You then draw two fresh cards, discard down to eight, and play again. Your hand next round is therefore mostly the cards that failed this round, so the decision on every strong card is whether this is the moment to cash it, knowing it will not be coming back.

This is the mastery loop made visible. In the first game, players spend their aces in round one and spend the next three rounds finding out why that was a mistake. Then they learn to win with the weakest card that will do the job, to lose deliberately with a king so it survives into a round where the other kings are gone, and to remember which high cards opponents still own, since nothing that has not won has left the game. The table's information grows each round, and by round four it is close to open-handed play among people who have been paying attention. The dynamic is a slow auction of strength with a self-correcting leader, and the feeling is thrift rewarded, the satisfaction of making a good meal out of what is left.

The mechanism needs only a deck of cards, the hook explains itself, and it runs from 2 to 4 unchanged. The scorer flags the theme and the late rounds. On theme, the same rule sits just as well on a vegetable patch where what you do not harvest reseeds, and I would test both covers with buyers. On pacing, the danger is that the leader coasts through round four with scraps while having nothing to do. I would try doubling points in the last round, and separately cutting to three rounds, and judge by whether the round-three leader wins less than two times in three.

### 8. Extinction Event — 71.2/100

*Four species start the game. One survives.*

- **Core twist:** Four rounds. Tricks score for the suit of the card that won them, tallied on a shared board. After each round the suit that won the fewest tricks goes extinct and all its cards leave the game, so each round is played with a smaller deck and shorter hands. Cards you hold of the surviving suit at the very end score double.
- **Theme:** Dinosaurs in their final days
- **Remixes:** The Six of VIII, Apocalypse, Rules or trumps that move on a visible schedule, A shared doom clock in the middle of the table
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Killing off the least-winning suit each round gives a strong shrinking-world arc and makes players champion suits rather than just tricks, helped by dinosaurs, but final rounds with two suits and tiny hands may be anticlimactic.

Extinction Event starts with four dinosaur suits and a small shared board with a tally for each. Hands are must-follow with no trump. Whenever a trick is won, its winner scores a point and the suit of the winning card advances on the board. At the end of the round, the suit that won the fewest tricks is extinct: every card of it is removed from the box, and the next round is dealt from the smaller deck. After the third round only one species remains for a short final hand, and any cards of the survivor in your last hand score double. The decision in each trick is double-edged: winning with a suit feeds its survival, so you want to win with the species you are holding and make everyone else's species lose.

What players learn is to think about suits as sides. A first game is played for tricks; the second is played for the board, once players see that a hand long in the doomed suit is a hand about to be worthless and that leading a suit in which a low card will win is a way to keep it alive. Interests overlap and shift each round as hands are redealt, so two players may quietly back the same species in round one and oppose each other in round two. The board makes all of this legible, and the shrinking deck gives the game a clear arc with a natural ending. The feeling is a story that narrows, and children in particular enjoy deciding which dinosaur they are trying to save.

Dinosaurs are as broad a theme as exists, the components are a deck and a tally board, and the rules are standard trick-taking plus one end-of-round step. It plays 2 to 4, with a three-card face-up row each at two. The risk is the anticlimax the scorer describes: a final round with one or two suits is hardly a trick-taking hand at all. I would playtest ending after three rounds with two survivors and a scoring bonus for cards of the leading species, and compare it with a version where extinct cards return as low wild fossils. ### 9. Twenty-One Tricks — 70.8/100

*Blackjack you play with tricks: get to twenty-one, not a pip more.*

- **Core twist:** Captured cards count their pips into a running total kept face-up in front of you. Closest to twenty-one without going over wins the round; over twenty-one is bust. When you cannot follow suit your discard lands in someone else's total, so sloughing a ten on the player sitting at fourteen is the whole game.
- **Theme:** A velvet-rope casino night
- **Remixes:** Schadenfreude, Trick to the Future, A hard score ceiling, Winning is dangerous: avoidance, bust and restraint scoring
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 7, mechanics 6, dynamics 8, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Blackjack with tricks is a hook any non-gamer grasps at once and dumping a ten on the player at fourteen is gleeful targeted play, but pip counting every capture and choosing whose pile a slough hits invite pile-ons and slow sums.

Twenty-One Tricks uses a deck where the pip value of each card is printed large: mostly ones to tens, as in the casino game. Play is must-follow with no trump. When you win a trick you add only the cards of the led suit to your face-up total; any card played off-suit is given by its player to the total of any opponent they choose. After the hands run out, or earlier if all but one player has stood, the closest to twenty-one without going over wins the round, and going over is a bust. A player may stand at the start of any trick, turning their remaining cards face-down and taking no further part. The central decision is when to stop winning, and when to stand before somebody drops a ten on you.

The pattern is blackjack's, but with the randomness replaced by other people. Players learn first that a total of fourteen is the most dangerous place to be, then that voids are the real weapons, since an off-suit card is a guided missile, and then that standing early with seventeen is often right because it removes you as a target. Counting which tens are still out matters and is easy because totals are public. Feedback is perfectly clear: you know exactly which card busted you and who threw it. The dynamics are targeted and noisy, with the table deciding together who is closest to the edge, and the feeling is casino tension without money.

The pitch is four words long and needs no explanation in any country, which is its commercial case; a deck and a few stand markers are all it costs, and it plays 2 to 4. The scorer's two risks are both real. For arithmetic, I would test a sliding marker on a zero-to-twenty-five strip for each player so nobody adds in their head. For pile-ons, I would test the rule that an off-suit card must go to the player who wins the trick rather than to anyone, which keeps the cruelty but makes it something you can see coming and dodge, and I would compare how often the same player is busted twice running under each rule.

### 10. Rope Team — 70.4/100

*Roped together, you reach the summit one after another or not at all.*

- **Core twist:** Cooperative. Players are roped in seat order and must win tricks in strict rotation: first climber, then the second, then the third, and round again. A trick won out of turn costs one of four oxygen tokens. Complete the number of in-order tricks on the mountain card before oxygen or cards run out.
- **Theme:** Alpine mountaineering
- **Remixes:** The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Potemkin Villages, A shared doom clock in the middle of the table
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 9, dynamics 6, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Win in strict seat rotation is a single rule the roped-climbers image explains completely and every failure is plainly attributable, but one fixed objective risks sameness unless the mountain cards add real variety.

Rope Team is cooperative. Players sit in rope order and a marker shows whose turn it is to win. Hands are dealt from a four-suit deck with one trump suit, and tricks are played by the usual must-follow rules, with nobody allowed to discuss their cards. If the marked climber wins the trick, the team moves one step up the mountain card and the marker passes to the next seat. If anyone else wins it, the team spends one of four oxygen tokens and the marker stays where it is. The mountain card says how many steps are needed, typically six or seven from nine tricks. The decision sits with everyone on every trick: the winner of the last trick must now lead something the next climber can win, and the others must stay out of the way.

What players learn is how to hand the lead across the table, which is the deepest skill in partnership card games, here stated as the entire goal. Early plays fail because someone leads their best suit; teams learn to lead low to the next climber's strength, to signal a strong suit by what they discard, and to save trumps for rescuing a trick that is going to the wrong person. Because failure is always a specific trick won by a specific wrong player, the lesson is unmissable and nobody needs it explained. The dynamic is silent coordination with a shared wince when it goes wrong, and the feeling is fellowship, the same thing that has made the cooperative trick-takers in this snapshot the most played.

The image of climbers on a rope carries the rule completely, the box is a deck, a dozen mountain cards and four tokens, and it scales from 2 to 4, using a face-up dummy climber at two. The scorer's worry is sameness, and I share it. The mountain cards are where the variety has to come from, and I would playtest a ladder of twelve: thin air where trumps cost oxygen, a traverse where the order reverses halfway, a storm where one climber plays blind, a summit where the last step must be won by the lowest card. The test is whether a group that beats mountain four wants to try five the same evening; if they stop, the cards are changing numbers and not the puzzle.
