# Trick-taking design analysis — 2026-11-14

Generated by a scheduled Claude Code session from the 20 most active games in the 2026-11-14 snapshot. 100 variants were generated and scored; the top 10 are pitched below. Scores are the model's judgement against a fixed rubric, useful for sorting ideas, not a forecast.

**Source games:** Schadenfreude, Apocalypse, Potemkin Villages, Minikin City, Moustache, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Can't Win, Trick to the Future, Fishing 2, Oshaka Trick, Off With Their Heads!, Zombie Princess, Matchmakers, Tax the Rich, Trick Stakes, Shards of Creation, Mythical Dice, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Seven Prophecies, The Six of VIII

## Top mechanics

- **Every finishing position pays** (Seven Prophecies, Schadenfreude, Off With Their Heads!, Minikin City): Seven Prophecies enters the set asking players to predict how often they will finish first, second and elsewhere, joining three games where coming second or last in a trick is a result with its own reward. Once every place matters, no card in the hand is dead and nobody waits out a trick they cannot win.
- **A bid plus a second promise** (Can't Win, Seven Prophecies, Mythical Dice, Tax the Rich): None of the bidding games here stops at a trick count. Each adds a second thing to commit to: a restriction, a spread of placings, a trump. The second promise is where the new pattern lives, and it lets a poor hand be bid cleverly instead of merely low.
- **Tricks you would rather not win** (Schadenfreude, Apocalypse, Oshaka Trick, Zombie Princess): Schadenfreude's plays rose again this week and it remains the hottest game in the set. Penalties, busts and poisoned tricks give low cards a job, keep the trailing player dangerous and turn someone's misfortune into the table's entertainment.
- **Cooperation with individual jobs** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Potemkin Villages): The Fellowship game logged over a thousand plays in the week, more than the next two games combined. A shared hand in which each seat has a different small task gives the feeling of a team without one player directing the rest, because the information needed to direct is hidden.
- **Partnerships that re-form** (Moustache, Tax the Rich, Zombie Princess, The Six of VIII, Matchmakers): Teams drawn fresh each round supply the pleasure of having a partner at any player count, and they dissolve grudges and runaway leads because nobody is on the same side twice running.
- **Captured cards are material, not just points** (Fishing 2, Matchmakers, Schadenfreude, Shards of Creation): When the contents of a trick are kept, matched, or played again, what the other players throw in becomes as important as who wins. That makes the off-suit discard, usually the dullest moment in a trick-taker, into an attack or a gift.
- **The trick drives a board, a sheet or a race** (Minikin City, Trick Stakes, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Off With Their Heads!): Using the trick as input to something visible gives newcomers a goal they can point at and gives the game a table presence a deck alone lacks. The trick stays quick; the consequences sit somewhere everyone can see.
- **One-line powers for characters and suits** (Potemkin Villages, Moustache, Zombie Princess, Shards of Creation, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game): A role or a suit with a single special rule gives identity and variety at low rules cost, and maps directly onto licensed characters. The designs that work keep each power short enough to read aloud once.
- **A resource that bends card values** (Trick to the Future, Minikin City): A small stock of tokens that changes what a card is worth gives players a lever against the deal and gives opponents something public to count. It moves skill from memory toward timing.

## Top themes

- **Prophecy, fate and time** (Seven Prophecies, Trick to the Future, Apocalypse): Prediction is what bidding already is, so fortune-telling and time-travel themes explain the central act of the game for free. This cluster grew this week and is a natural home for any design about foresight.
- **Literary and licensed fantasy** (The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Shards of Creation, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Off With Their Heads!, Zombie Princess): The two most-owned games in the set both borrow a story the buyer already knows. A familiar cast lowers the barrier for people who would not pick up an abstract card game.
- **The macabre played for laughs** (Apocalypse, Zombie Princess, Oshaka Trick, Schadenfreude, Dracula vs Van Helsing): Hellish contests, zombies and gloating go with rules that punish, because the theme promises in advance that bad things will happen and that it will be funny when they do.
- **Thrones, class and politics** (The Six of VIII, Tax the Rich, Potemkin Villages, Off With Their Heads!): Card ranks are a social ladder already. Themes about who outranks whom, and about that order being overturned, let the fiction carry both the basic rule and the twist.
- **Animals and the outdoors** (Moustache, Fishing 2, Matchmakers): Warm, funny animal settings carry chaotic or competitive rules to family tables. Only three of twenty games use them, which leaves room.
- **Stakes, races and spectacle** (Trick Stakes, Mythical Dice, Seven Prophecies): A race or a wager makes the state of the game visible and gives every trick an audience, which helps players who do not track tricks in their heads.
- **Building something small** (Minikin City, Potemkin Villages): Ending the game with something constructed in front of you gives a sense of ownership that a face-down pile of tricks cannot.

## Top differentiators

- **Predicting your placings, not only your wins** (Seven Prophecies): The one new entrant asks how many times you will come first, second and other. It widens bidding from one number to a profile of the hand, and makes second place in a trick something to plan for.
- **Second-highest takes the trick** (Schadenfreude): One altered sentence about who wins the trick resets everyone's instincts, so a table of mixed experience starts even. It is the clearest proof in the set that a single clean rule change can carry a game.
- **A handicap you choose yourself** (Can't Win, Seven Prophecies): Choosing your own restriction or prediction after seeing your cards turns the luck of the deal into a decision the player owns, including when it fails.
- **Shared doom tokens** (Apocalypse, Potemkin Villages): A common countdown in the middle of the table makes rivals nervous together and creates brinkmanship with no player attacking another.
- **Hands and catches that carry over** (Trick Stakes, Fishing 2): Playing the same hand again, or playing with what you caught, lets players get to know their cards across rounds. Learning is visible from one round to the next, which is exactly the loop Koster describes.
- **Rule changes announced in advance** (Moustache, The Six of VIII, Shards of Creation, Zombie Princess): A timeline of trumps, a new rule each round or a different mix of suits each game renews the puzzle on a schedule everyone can see, so variety feels planned for and not arbitrary.
- **A score you must not pass, and scores that multiply** (Schadenfreude, Trick to the Future, Can't Win): A ceiling makes the leader the most exposed player; a multiplier makes one neglected factor ruin a good round. Both give late tricks a second meaning and act as catch-up without a catch-up rule.
- **Honest play at two** (Dracula vs Van Helsing, Seven Prophecies, Minikin City, Potemkin Villages, Matchmakers, Shards of Creation, Off With Their Heads!, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Trick to the Future): Nine of the twenty now list two players. The form traditionally needs three or four, and designs that work for a couple without a dummy hand reach the largest group of buyers.
- **Props beyond the deck** (Mythical Dice, Trick Stakes, Off With Their Heads!, Apocalypse, Trick to the Future): Dice, a race track, a score sheet, tokens or fuel rods give a small card game a recognisable look and something to handle, provided the box stays small.

### 1. Thin Ice — 73.7/100

*Each colour of ice holds three winners. The fourth goes through.*

- **Core twist:** Every suit has an ice-floe card with three crack spaces. When a trick is won, a crack is marked on the floe matching the winning card's suit. A player who wins a trick with a suit whose floe already has three cracks falls in: they lose three points and the floe resets. Tricks are otherwise a point each.
- **Theme:** Penguins on melting ice
- **Remixes:** Apocalypse, Oshaka Trick, Shared doom tokens, Tricks you would rather not win, Animals and the outdoors
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Three cracks per suit then the fourth winner falls in is visible, countable and teaches itself, with penguins to sell it at 2-4 players; the risk is that late rounds reduce to forcing a known victim onto a cracked floe.

Thin Ice is a must-follow trick-taker with four suits, no trump and nine tricks a round. Each suit has an ice-floe card beside the table with three spaces for crack tokens. Whenever a trick is won, a crack goes on the floe of the winning card's suit. Tricks are a point each. The fourth time a suit wins, there is no space left: the winner falls through, loses three points, and the floe is cleared. The central decision arrives as soon as a floe shows three cracks. Leading that suit is now an attack, holding its high cards is a liability, and the player who can duck under everyone else is safe while the player holding the ace is being hunted.

What players learn is to read the floes as a second trick count. The first lesson is to get rid of high cards in a suit before it reaches three cracks. The second is that a cracked suit can be led low to force out exactly the card you know someone is stuck with, and the third is that falling in on purpose is sometimes right, because it resets the floe and makes your remaining cards in that suit worth winning with again. Because all of it is counted in tokens on the table, a child can follow why they fell in, and the pattern holds interest because four floes at different depths make each hand a different map. At the table it produces the gleeful ganging-up of a hot-potato game without anyone being singled out by choice: the ice chooses the victim.

Penguins, a deck, four floe cards and a handful of tokens make a cheap box with an obvious cover, and the rule reads in one sentence. It plays 2 to 4; at two, each player also plays the top card of a small face-down stack so tricks have three cards. The scorer's concern is that late tricks become mechanical once the victim is known. I would playtest two remedies: letting a player who cannot follow suit discard face-down, so the count of what is still out is uncertain, and paying one point to whoever led the trick that sinks someone, so that setting it up is rewarded. The measure is how often the player who falls in could have avoided it two tricks earlier; if the answer is rarely, the hand needs more cards per suit.

### 2. Torch and Treasure — 72.7/100

*Win the trick and you face the monster with the very card you won with.*

- **Core twist:** Each trick is a room card showing a monster's strength from three to nine and a treasure. The trick winner compares their winning card to the monster: equal or higher takes the treasure; lower takes a wound worth minus one, and the monster stays for the next trick with more treasure on it. A cheap win is a trap.
- **Theme:** A storybook dungeon
- **Remixes:** Oshaka Trick, Dracula vs Van Helsing, Off With Their Heads!, Tricks you would rather not win, Literary and licensed fantasy
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.7
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Having to beat the room's monster with the very card that won makes 'a cheap win is a trap' obvious and fun, with a widely loved theme at 2-4; the risk is a strong monster nobody can beat stalling several tricks.

Torch and Treasure is played with an ordinary four-suit deck ranked 1 to 10 and a small deck of room cards. Before each trick a room is turned up showing a monster with a strength from three to nine and a treasure worth one to three points. The trick is played by must-follow rules with one trump suit. The winner then faces the monster with the card they won with: if its rank equals or beats the monster's strength they take the treasure, and if not they take a wound worth minus one, and the monster stays for the next trick with another point of treasure added. So the question on every card is not only whether it will win, but whether it is big enough to deserve to.

This gives players a new thing to learn about a familiar act. Trumping in with a 2 wins the trick and loses the fight, so small trumps become a way to duck, not to steal. Leading low into a strong monster forces somebody to take a wound, and a monster that has survived twice is carrying enough treasure to be worth spending an ace on, which everyone can see coming. Over repeated plays the skill is in matching your hand to the row of rooms: knowing you hold only two cards that can beat a nine and saving them. Feedback is exact, since the comparison is two numbers side by side. The dynamic is a table daring one another to go into the room, and the feeling is a light dungeon crawl in which the bold player is sometimes simply wrong.

A friendly dungeon is among the most reliable themes in the hobby and is well understood outside it, the rule is one comparison, and it runs from 2 to 4 with only a second small deck added to the box. The risk the scorer names is a monster nobody can beat stalling the game. I would test a rule that a monster leaves after its third trick and wounds whoever won that trick, and a gentler option where two players' cards may be added if they are the two highest of the led suit. I would also check the spread of strengths against a ten-card suit so that roughly a third of all winning cards fail, which is where the tension felt best in my first paper pass.

### 3. Again! — 72.6/100

*Fail the hand, pick up the very same cards, and try it again knowing what you know.*

- **Core twist:** Cooperative. A goal card sets a task, such as 'everyone wins exactly two tricks and a 1 wins the last'. If the team fails, every player picks their exact hand back up and the same deal is replayed, up to three attempts, with no discussion in between. Score by how few attempts you needed.
- **Theme:** A morning stuck in a time loop
- **Remixes:** Trick Stakes, Trick to the Future, The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring – Trick-Taking Game, Hands and catches that carry over, Prophecy, fate and time
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.7
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 9, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 9, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 6
- **Scorer's note:** Replaying the identical deal after a failure is the purest Koster loop on the list, turning hidden information into learned information, but it leans on memory, needs a strong goal deck and repeats hands that a casual group may find tiring.

Again! is a cooperative game of very short sessions. A goal card is turned up, for example 'each of you wins exactly two tricks, and the last trick is won by a 1'. Eight cards are dealt to each player and the hand is played by must-follow rules with no discussion of cards. If the goal is met, the team scores three stars. If not, every player picks their own eight cards back up, nobody says anything, and the identical deal is played again; success now scores two stars, and on a third and last attempt, one. Cards are played in front of their owners so that hands can be recovered. The decision in the first attempt is how much to reveal by your play; in the second it is how to use what you now know about where everything is.

This is Koster's loop with the lid off. The first attempt is exploration, the second is the same puzzle with most of the hidden information converted into memory, and the pleasure is in feeling that conversion happen. Teams learn to treat a doomed first attempt as reconnaissance, playing to show partners their long suit or where the low cards are. They learn that the lead in trick one is the lever, since it is the only thing that can be changed with certainty. The pattern stays fresh because the goal deck changes what is worth finding out. At the table it produces a distinctive silence followed by a rush of recognition, and the feeling is the one the time-loop theme promises: this time we know what happens next.

Cooperative trick-taking is the proven mainstream route in this snapshot, and this needs only a deck and forty goal cards. It plays 2 to 4, with two players each managing an extra face-up hand of four. The scorer is right that memory and repetition are the risks. I would test whether keeping each player's first-attempt cards in played order as a visible record removes the memory burden without removing the puzzle, and I would cap a session at five goals so nobody plays the same deal for long. The key measure is the second-attempt success rate: near forty percent the game should feel earned, and much above sixty the goals are too easy to need the loop.

### 4. Line Up — 71.3/100

*Win a trick, drop a disc in that suit's column, and make three in a row.*

- **Core twist:** A shared upright grid has one column per suit, five spaces high. The trick winner drops one of their discs into the column of the winning card's suit. At the end, each line of three of your discs in any direction scores three. Which suit you win with is the move.
- **Theme:** A bright abstract disc-drop game
- **Remixes:** Dracula vs Van Helsing, Minikin City, The trick drives a board, a sheet or a race, Props beyond the deck
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 7, aesthetics 6, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** A disc-drop grid with one column per suit borrows a pattern everyone already knows and makes the winning suit a visible move with blocking, though the upright grid raises cost and the abstract theme adds no warmth.

Line Up stands a small grid in the middle of the table, four columns wide and five high, with each column marked in one suit's colour. Players have discs in their own colour. The cards are ordinary: four suits, must-follow, no trump, eight tricks a hand and two hands a game. When you win a trick you drop one of your discs into the column of the suit you won with, and it falls to the lowest free space. At the end, every line of three of your discs, across, up or diagonally, scores three points, and tricks score nothing else. The decision is that a trick is no longer just worth winning or not: it is worth winning in red, or worth denying to someone in blue.

Players arrive already knowing how to read the grid, so the new pattern is the connection between hand and board. They learn that a suit they are long in is a column they can stack, that a full column is a dead suit nobody wants to win with, and that the cheapest block is to win a trick in the colour an opponent needs with a card they could have beaten in any other suit. Because off-suit cards cannot win, running someone out of a colour locks them out of that column. Every result is visible and stays visible, which makes it easy to see what went wrong. The table dynamic is open blocking and counter-blocking, and the feeling is the familiar satisfaction of spotting a line a move before your opponent does.

The pitch needs no trick-taking vocabulary at all, which is its reach: people who would never sit down to a card game will recognise the grid. It plays 2 to 4. The scorer flags cost and the lack of a warm theme. On cost, I would prototype a flat board with a sliding marker per column in place of an upright frame and check that players still read lines easily. On theme, beads on an abacus or fruit in a crate would dress it without touching the rule. The design risk to test is at four players, where twenty spaces for sixteen discs may make lines too rare; I would compare a six-high grid and lines of three scoring two.

### 5. Breakaway — 71.2/100

*Follow suit and you ride your card's number. Fail to follow and you stand still.*

- **Core twist:** Each player has a cyclist on a road. After a trick, every player who followed suit moves forward by the rank of the card they played; anyone who played off-suit does not move. The winner leads, so the lead is the power to pick a suit your rivals have run out of. Play eight of ten cards.
- **Theme:** A mountain stage of a bike race
- **Remixes:** Trick Stakes, Minikin City, Seven Prophecies, The trick drives a board, a sheet or a race, Stakes, races and spectacle
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Moving by the rank you played only if you followed suit turns voids into a liability and the lead into an attack, and cycling fits it well; the risk is that high cards both win and move furthest, so strong hands run away.

Breakaway puts a road of forty spaces on the table and a cyclist for each player. Hands are ten cards from a four-suit deck ranked 1 to 9, and each round is eight tricks, so two cards are never played. After each trick, every player who followed suit moves their rider forward by the rank of the card they played, and anyone who played off-suit stays where they are. The trick winner gains nothing except the lead. That lead is the weapon: lead the suit a rival has run out of and they stand still while you ride. The central decision is whether to spend a high card to move far now, or a lower one to keep control of a suit for later.

The lesson that develops is that suit length is speed. A hand with five cards in one suit can lead it repeatedly and move every time, while opponents run dry and stop. Players learn to count who is short where, to burn off a suit early so they are not dragged along slowly in it, and to choose which two cards to leave unplayed. They also learn that winning a trick with a 9 moves you nine but following with an 8 moves you eight, so the fight is for the lead far more than for the trick. The race track makes cause and effect plain: you did not move because you had no green. At the table it creates attacks, counter-attacks and stranded riders, which is what a bike race looks like.

A race is the easiest scoring system to explain to a non-gamer, cycling has a wide following in exactly the markets where trick-taking is strongest, and the game works at 2 to 4 with a track and four pawns. The scorer's worry is that high cards both win and move furthest, so a strong hand runs away. I would playtest two fixes: the leader's own card moves two spaces fewer, as if riding into the wind, and climbs marked on the road where movement is capped at five. I would deal a few hundred hands in a spreadsheet first to see how closely final position follows the sum of the hand, and keep adjusting until play order matters more than the deal.

### 6. Monster Mash-Up — 71.2/100

*Heads, bodies and legs: win tricks to stitch together the best monsters.*

- **Core twist:** The three suits are heads, bodies and legs. The trick winner takes one card from the trick and adds it to one of up to three monsters in their lab, each of which needs one of each part. A complete monster scores the lowest number among its three parts; an incomplete one scores nothing.
- **Theme:** A cartoon monster laboratory
- **Remixes:** Matchmakers, Minikin City, Zombie Princess, Captured cards are material, not just points, Building something small
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 6, learning feedback 8, mechanics 8, dynamics 6, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Heads, bodies and legs with a monster scoring its lowest part is visual, child-friendly and works at 2-4, and choosing which card to take is a clear decision; the risk is three suits giving thin card play for experienced players.

In Monster Mash-Up the deck has three suits, heads, bodies and legs, each numbered 1 to 12 and each card illustrated as that part of a different monster. Play is must-follow with no trump, ten tricks a round. The winner of a trick takes exactly one card from it, any of the cards played, and puts it in their lab as part of one of up to three monsters, each of which needs one head, one body and one pair of legs. The rest of the trick is discarded. At the end of the round a complete monster scores the lowest number among its three parts and an incomplete one scores nothing. The decision is which card to take: the 11 head that needs two more good parts, or the 5 legs that finishes something now.

The scoring rule teaches balance. A monster of 12, 12 and 2 scores two, so high cards are only as good as their weakest partner, and players quickly learn to build level creatures and to start no more than they can finish. Because you take a card from the trick, what opponents play becomes your raw material: following with a low body is safe, and discarding a high head on a trick your rival will win is a gift you should have thought about. Labs are public, so everyone can see who needs legs. The dynamic is light denial and a lot of holding up of absurd creatures, and the feeling is expression: the game leaves you with three mismatched monsters that you made.

It is a mix-and-match picture book with a trick-taking engine, which is the kind of object that sells on sight to families, and it needs nothing beyond 36 well-illustrated cards. It plays 2 to 4 unchanged. The scorer's risk is that three suits make the card play thin for experienced players. I would test a fourth suit of accessories that adds its number to any finished monster, and a rule that the winner may instead take the top card of the discard, to see which adds decisions without lengthening the teach. I would watch specifically whether adults playing without children ask for a second game.

### 7. Kickflip — 71.0/100

*One trick is a point. Two in a row is three. Keep the run going.*

- **Core twist:** Tricks score by streak: the first trick of a run is worth one point, the next consecutive win two, the next three, and so on. Losing a trick resets you to one. Whoever is on a run becomes the player everyone must stop, even at the cost of their own good cards.
- **Theme:** A skate park
- **Remixes:** Can't Win, Oshaka Trick, A score you must not pass, and scores that multiply
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.5
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 5, mastery depth 5, learning feedback 8, mechanics 9, dynamics 7, aesthetics 7, mainstream reach 8
- **Scorer's note:** Streak scoring is explained in one breath, works at 2-4 and creates an obvious stop-the-leader drama, but a strong hand running five tricks scores fifteen and can decide the game on the deal.

Kickflip is trick-taking with one change to the score sheet. Hands are nine cards, must-follow, with a trump suit turned from the deck. Your first trick in a run scores one point, a second straight away scores two, a third three, and the run ends the moment someone else wins. Each player has a marker on a small combo track so the table can see what the next trick would be worth to whom. The decision sits in two places: when you hold the lead, whether to cash a sure winner now or set up a longer run for later, and when you do not, whether to spend your good card stopping someone else's third trick or keep it for a run of your own.

The lesson is sequencing. Three tricks won apart score three points; won together they score six. Players learn to hold their winners back until they can be played in a row, to keep a low card to hand the lead away at a moment of their choosing, and to judge which opponent's run is worth breaking. That last judgement is shared: breaking a run costs the breaker a card, and the players not on the run each hope the other will pay. The combo track makes all of this legible, and the dynamic is a rolling stop-the-leader contest with a different leader every few tricks. The feeling is momentum, the same pleasure as landing a string of tricks in a skateboarding video game.

The rule is one breath long, the theme is young and under-used in card games, and the game needs a deck and a score track; it plays 2 to 4. The scorer names the real danger: a dealt hand that runs five tricks scores fifteen and settles the game. I would playtest capping a run at three before it resets, and separately scoring a point to whoever breaks a run of two or more, which pays the table for doing the work. I would also try shortening hands to seven cards. The measure is how often the round's top scorer was also dealt the most high cards; that should be well under half.

### 8. Talent Show — 70.9/100

*The cards that win the heats are the only cards you get to play in the final.*

- **Core twist:** Six heat tricks are played from nine cards and score a point each. The card that wins a heat stays face-up in front of its owner as a finalist. After the heats, all other cards are discarded and the final is played using only finalists as hands, at three points a trick. A heat won with a small card sends a weak act to the final.
- **Theme:** A village talent contest
- **Remixes:** Fishing 2, Trick Stakes, Hands and catches that carry over, Stakes, races and spectacle
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.7
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 7, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 8, mechanics 7, dynamics 6, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Heat-winning cards becoming your hand for a triple-value final makes how you win matter and tells a clear two-act story, but a player with no finalists watches the final and uneven final hands need a rule.

Talent Show is a round in two acts. Each player is dealt nine cards. The first six tricks are heats, played must-follow with one trump suit and worth a point each. The card that wins a heat is not gathered: it stays face-up in front of its owner as a finalist. After six heats, everything else is discarded and the final begins, played only with finalists as hands, at three points a trick. The decision in every heat is which card to win with. Take a heat with your ace of trump and it will be waiting for you in the final; sneak one with a 4 because nobody else could follow, and you have sent a 4 to the final.

What players learn is that a win has a quality as well as a value. The early games teach the obvious, to win heats with big cards. Later ones teach that a single very strong finalist is worth more than three weak ones, that the suits of the finalists on the table decide who can follow whom in the final, and that deliberately losing a heat to keep an awkward low card out of your final hand is good play. The finalists are face-up, so the final is close to open information and rewards having planned for it. The round has a clear arc, with the table watching the line-up for the final take shape, and the feeling is anticipation followed by a short, sharp payoff.

The structure is familiar to anyone who has watched a talent contest, the components are a single deck, and it is built for 3 to 4 players. The scorer points out that a player with no finalists watches the final and that uneven final hands need a rule. ### 9. Last One Out — 70.7/100

*Leave the party whenever you like, but the last guest does the washing-up.*

- **Core twist:** After any trick, a player may leave: they discard their hand and bank a point for each trick they have won. When only one player remains the round ends and that player scores nothing. With two players left, every further trick is a dare.
- **Theme:** A house party winding down
- **Remixes:** Oshaka Trick, Schadenfreude, Tricks you would rather not win, A score you must not pass, and scores that multiply
- **Players / weight:** 3–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 5, learning feedback 7, mechanics 9, dynamics 8, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Bank-and-leave with the last player scoring nothing is a pure, readable game of chicken with a theme everyone gets, but the final two players face a near-solved stare-down and early leavers sit idle.

Last One Out is a ten-trick hand of ordinary must-follow trick-taking with one extra option. After any trick, before the next lead, any player may announce that they are leaving the party. They discard their hand and bank one point for every trick they have won. Play continues among those still in. When only one player remains, the round ends immediately and that player scores nothing at all. The decision is the same at every pause and gets harder each time: I have three tricks and a hand that could win two more, but if the player opposite leaves before I do, and then the next one, I am the one left holding the washing-up.

Players learn to read exits as well as cards. The first lesson is that a weak hand should leave early with a point or two. The next is that every departure changes the game for those remaining, both by removing cards from play and by bringing the last chair closer, and that the strongest hand at the table is the one most likely to be stranded because it has the most reason to stay. At three players remaining the calculation is delicate, since the first of the three to go leaves a pure duel behind. The dynamic is a table watching one another over the tops of their cards, and the feeling is the suspense of a game of chicken in which nobody is attacked: you were not pushed, you stayed.

It costs a deck of cards, the theme is one everybody has lived, and the rule is two sentences, for 3 to 4 players. The scorer's two risks are a solved ending and idle early leavers. For the ending, I would test simultaneous exits, with each remaining player secretly choosing stay or go after every trick and revealing together, so the last two cannot simply react to each other; if both go, both bank. For idleness, rounds are five minutes, but I would also test letting a player who has left bet one point on who will be last. The measure is whether the final two ever play more than two tricks; if they never do, the stare-down is solved.

### 10. Nocturnal — 70.0/100

*When a moon card wins, night falls and low cards rule until the sun comes up.*

- **Core twist:** The round begins in daylight, when high cards win. The 3s and 8s carry a moon and the 5s and 10s a sun. When a moon card wins a trick it becomes night, and the lowest card of the led suit wins every trick until a sun card wins one. The players decide when the world turns over.
- **Theme:** Owls and larks in a forest
- **Remixes:** Moustache, The Six of VIII, Schadenfreude, Rule changes announced in advance, Animals and the outdoors
- **Players / weight:** 2–4, 1.6
- **Scores:** pattern novelty 6, mastery depth 7, learning feedback 7, mechanics 7, dynamics 7, aesthetics 8, mainstream reach 7
- **Scorer's note:** Moon and sun cards that flip the whole ranking when they win give players control over when low cards rule and make every hand playable, though the trigger depends on those specific ranks actually winning, which can be hard to arrange.

Nocturnal uses four suits ranked 1 to 10 and a single double-sided tile showing day on one face and night on the other. The round starts in daylight, with normal must-follow rules and the highest card of the led suit winning. The 3s and 8s have a moon printed on them and the 5s and 10s a sun. When a moon card wins a trick, the tile is flipped to night, and from the next trick the lowest card of the led suit wins. Night lasts until a sun card wins a trick, which flips the tile back. Tricks score a point each. The decision is about timing the turn: a hand of low cards wants a long night and must get a moon card to win, and by day the 8 is the moon most likely to manage it.

The asymmetry between the four special ranks is what there is to learn. By day an 8 wins fairly often and a 3 almost never; by night a 5 wins sometimes and a 10 only when everyone else is out of the suit. So dusk is usually brought on by an 8 and dawn by a 5, and players come to treat those eight cards as the switches of the game, tracking which have gone. A hand is no longer good or bad but good by day or good by night, and the skill is in getting the tile onto your side for as many tricks as possible. The tile makes the state unmistakable. The dynamic is a tug over the light switch, and the feeling is the small thrill of the whole table's hands changing value at once.

The idea of high-wins and low-wins phases is easy for anyone who has played a card game, owls and larks give it a gentle look, and it needs one deck and one tile for 2 to 4 players. The scorer's risk is that the switches depend on particular ranks happening to win. I would test making the flip happen when a moon or sun card is played by the trick's winner or by the player of the lowest card, which roughly doubles how often it can be arranged, and track flips per round. Between two and four feels right; fewer and the game is ordinary, more and nobody can plan.
